Book proposal · working outline

The 2D Renderer Challenge

A test-driven book that builds a complete 2D vector renderer from nothing — lines, curves, fills, strokes, gradients, clips and type — in whatever language you like, writing every pixel yourself.

Model · The Ray Tracer Challenge Output · PPM files only Parts · 6 + 2 bonus tracks Chapters · 24 Finale · The Ghostscript tiger
Frontispiece · an edge, and the coverage it produces magenta = the geometry · gray = the picture

Every chapter answers one question in two halves: what fraction of this pixel does the shape cover, and what paint do I push through that coverage. Fills, strokes, clips and glyphs are all the same machine wearing different hats.

The through-line

One idea, held for twenty-four chapters

The Ray Tracer Challenge works because it has a single load-bearing sentence — intersect a ray with a thing, then shade the hit — and every chapter after the fifth is a variation on it. A 2D book needs the same kind of through-line or it degenerates into a bag of algorithms.

The sentence here is rasterization computes coverage; painting composites paint through coverage. Once the reader holds a coverage buffer in their hands, a stroke is a fill of a different outline, a clip is a multiplication of two coverage buffers, and a glyph is a path someone else authored. Three-quarters of the book is teaching the reader to see that.

The second reason it works is that each chapter ends in a picture. That is even more true in 2D: there is no scene to set up, no camera to place. Every plate on this page is something the reader can put on screen by the end of its chapter, and every one of them is obviously right or obviously broken.

Ground rules

What the reader is and isn't allowed to lean on

How it's written

The rhythm of a chapter

Everything above is what the book covers. This is the part that decides whether anyone finishes it. The Ray Tracer Challenge works as much for its cadence and its manners as for its curriculum, and those deserve to be specified as tightly as the chapter list.

  1. Explain the idea in plain words.

    A few paragraphs, with a line drawing if the idea is geometric. No notation the reader hasn't met, and nothing introduced that isn't used within the page.

  2. Show the test.

    A Cucumber scenario that pins exactly the behavior just described. The reader knows what done looks like before writing a line of code, which is the single biggest reason this format keeps people moving.

  3. Let them write it.

    The book does not print the implementation. It prints the explanation, the test, and the shape of the answer. The code is theirs, in their language, and that ownership is the whole point.

  4. Run it and look at it.

    Dump a PPM and open it. Not only at the end of the chapter — after most steps. A wrong picture is more informative than a failing assertion, and far more fun.

  5. Repeat five to eight times, then assemble.

    The pieces come together into the chapter's payoff image. The unit of work is a sitting: an evening, two or three hours, for someone who is also making dinner. A chapter that can't be finished in one is two chapters.

§  Small steps, pinned by tests

Concept, test, code — interleaved, never stacked. The reader should not encounter more than about a page of prose before there is something to type, and never a wall of theory followed by an exercise. Scenarios are small enough that a failing one names the bug on its own.

Feature: Coverage

  Scenario: An edge straight down the middle of a pixel
    Given e ← edge(point(3, 0), point(3, 10))
    When  c ← coverage(e, pixel(2, 4))
    Then  c = 0.5

  Scenario: Exact coverage for an axis-aligned rectangle
    Given r ← rectangle(1.25, 2.0, 4.75, 5.0)
    When  cov ← rasterize(r)
    Then  cov[1,2] = 0.75    # partial column, left edge
    And   cov[2,2] = 1.0     # interior
    And   cov[4,2] = 0.75    # partial column, right edge
    And   cov[5,2] = 0.0     # outside

  Scenario: A curve is only ever approximately covered
    Given c ← circle(point(8, 8), 5)
    When  cov ← rasterize(c)
    Then  cov[3,8] = 0.5 ± 0.001   # tier two: tolerance, not equality

Two practical notes. Tolerances are written into the scenario itself, in the open, so nobody wonders whether a test is fudged — the convention is set out once, in chapter 1's How to read a test, and the three tiers are described under Testing. Gherkin is readable without a runner, and plenty of languages have no Cucumber, so the book presumes the reader translates each scenario into their own test framework by hand. Ray Tracer Challenge readers already do this; the book admits it up front.

§  The "In the GUI" asides

The reader has just built something that already exists in software they use every week. Naming the exact control it hides behind converts an algorithm into recognition — oh, that's what that does — and that feeling is most of the reason people finish books like this. It is a reward, not a lesson.

Good — chapter 13

The three cap icons and three join icons in every stroke panel, in this order. Object → Path → Outline Stroke in Illustrator runs this function and replaces the path with its result — which is a good way to check your own output against a shipping implementation.

Bad — the version to avoid

Most graphics applications have options controlling how lines end and how corners are drawn. You have probably seen these in your favorite vector editor. Try experimenting with them to see the different effects you can achieve!

Rules
  • Sixty words, hard limit. If it needs more, it's a chapter section, not an aside.
  • Name a real control by its actual label and menu path. "Most editors have a setting" is worth nothing.
  • Prefer the control that exposes the exact parameter just implemented — miter limit, dash phase, fill rule, tolerance.
  • Never load-bearing. A reader who skips every one finishes the book with the same renderer.
  • End in recognition, not homework. No "try experimenting with".
  • One app, usually. A second only when the difference in naming is itself interesting.
Treatment
  • Boxed and tinted, with a small monospaced label — visibly a detour from the body text.
  • Placed after the work, never mid-derivation. It interrupts nothing.
  • Icons redrawn, not screenshotted where the control has a recognizable one: the three cap glyphs, the Pathfinder row. Screenshots date within a release; line drawings don't.
  • No branding, no version numbers, no vendor enthusiasm.

§  The picture at the end

Every chapter terminates in an image the reader made, printed at full size so they can compare. It is the recap — there is no summary section, no bullet-point review, no "what we learned". The plate is what they learned.

What qualifies
  • Impossible one chapter earlier. If it would have rendered before this chapter's code existed, pick a different image.
  • Worth looking at. A clock face and a lit sphere, not a grid of test squares. If the reader wouldn't post it, it isn't the payoff.
  • Reproducible. Ships with the short program that generates it and any scene data it needs.
  • Numbered as a plate, so later chapters can refer back — "the rasterizer you wrote for Plate 7".
How the chapter closes
  • A "putting it together" program of thirty lines or fewer, typed in one go.
  • Then the plate. Then the chapter stops. Resist the urge to explain it.
  • Anything unfinished becomes an exercise with a picture of its own attached, so it stays tempting rather than nagging.

§  Voice

Casual, funny, and generous — a competent friend explaining something over a beer, not a paper. The material is genuinely intricate in places, and the prose has to carry the reader through that without ever making them feel slow. Short sentences. Then a long one when it earns the room.

Never
  • simply · just · obviously · trivially · of course · clearly
    Every one of them tells a stuck reader that the problem is them.
  • !  Exclamation marks used to manufacture enthusiasm the sentence hasn't earned.
  • we  when it means you. The reader is doing the work; give them the credit.
  • Formulas that arrive from nowhere. Derive it or cite it, but never assert it.
  • Calling your own design elegant. Let the reader decide.
Always
  • Second person and contractions. You're building this.
  • Jokes out of the material, not bolted onto it. The flat-topped polygon that sprouts spikes. The seam every renderer on earth has and nobody mentions. The fact that no one can offset a Bézier and the whole industry quietly rounds it off.
  • Admit when something is horrible. The arc flag conversion is miserable; say so. A reader only believes "this next part is easy" from an author who has been honest about the hard parts.
  • Name the concept after the reader needs it. Winding numbers arrive once the pentagram has already gone wrong, never before.
  • Open on the problem, concretely, ideally with a picture of it failing. Never on an abstract.
Part I

Pixels and Coverage

Three chapters to establish the vocabulary, and to get one deliberately bad renderer on the page so that everything afterwards has something to beat.

01

The Canvas and the Color

A float RGB triple, a 2D buffer of them, and a PPM writer. Then, immediately, the trap that the rest of the book depends on: the numbers in an image file are not proportional to light. Encode and decode sRGB, and blend two colors both ways to see the difference.

Builds
Color, Canvas (storing linear light), fill, canvas_to_ppm (encoding on the way out), sRGB transfer functions, mix with a linear-blending switch, ppm_pixel and max_channel_difference for reading files back in tests.
Insight
Storage is perceptual, arithmetic is physical. Averaging encoded values averages the wrong quantity.
Trap
Doing it correctly makes your output look different from every browser, which blend in encoded space. The book must pick a lane loudly in chapter 1 rather than apologize in chapter 9.
Tests
Exact scalar checks on the transfer functions on both sides of both knees; PPM output byte-for-byte including the 70-column wrap; five small renders (the gray match, one-in-four, a 256-step ramp, a clamp pair, and the plate) pinned by pixel values and diffed against shipped reference PPMs with a budget of 1.

In the GUI

This is the setting behind Photoshop's Blend RGB Colors Using Gamma 1.00 checkbox and Figma's blend-space choice. It is also why a 50% gray brush over black looks lighter in one app than another.

02

Coverage

Draw a disc by asking each pixel a question. First the binary one, then the useful one, answered by brute force: throw 64 sample points at the pixel and count. The result is slow, correct, and becomes the reference renderer that every later chapter is diffed against.

Builds
A coverage buffer, a supersampling evaluator, and paint_through(coverage, color).
Insight
Antialiasing is not a smoothing pass applied afterwards. It is what you get when you ask a better question up front.
Trap
Coverage and opacity are different quantities that both live in the alpha channel, and conflating them causes the seam artifact in chapter 9.
Tests
Coverage of a half-plane through a pixel center is 0.5 ± 1/64; a pixel fully inside is 1.0 exactly.
03

Lines

Bresenham first, because it is beautiful, integer-only, and forty years of graphics folklore. Then Xiaolin Wu's version, which looks enormously better. Then the reveal: Wu's weights are approximate coverage of a one-pixel-wide rectangle, and the general answer is to stop special-casing lines and fill a thin quadrilateral instead.

Builds
line_bresenham, line_wu, and the fan generator used for the plate.
Insight
Special-purpose primitives are a local optimum. The book spends one chapter there so it can leave on purpose.
Trap
Wu's endpoint handling is where every implementation quietly breaks; near-horizontal and near-vertical cases need explicit tests.
Tests
A 45° line has uniform weights; a horizontal line has weight 1 on one row and 0 on its neighbor; total ink is invariant under angle.

In the GUI

The pencil versus the brush. A 1px pencil in any raster editor is still Bresenham with the antialiasing switched off, which is exactly why pixel artists reach for it.

Part II

Geometry and the Fill

The core engine. By the end of chapter 8 the reader has a rasterizer that is competitive with production ones, and everything after this is feeding it more interesting shapes.

04

Points, Vectors, Transforms

3×3 homogeneous matrices, the affine subset, composition and inversion. Crucially: decomposition, because later chapters need the scale factor pulled back out of a matrix to compute flattening tolerance and stroke width in device space.

Builds
Matrix3, translate/scale/rotate/skew, inverse, and approx_scale().
Insight
A transform is not a property of a shape. It is a change of the coordinate system the shape is described in, and it propagates into tolerance.
Trap
Non-uniform scale makes a circular pen elliptical. Deciding whether your strokes live in user space or device space is a real design decision, and SVG's vector-effect exists because of it.
Tests
Straight from The Ray Tracer Challenge's playbook: inverse round-trips, chained transforms applied in reverse, and known point images.
05

Paths and Insideness

Move-to, line-to, close, subpaths. Then the question that has no single right answer: given a point and a self-intersecting path, is the point inside? Ray casting, crossing counts, winding numbers, and the two rules every graphics system offers.

Builds
Path, subpath iteration, winding_at(), path bounding box.
Insight
"Inside" is a convention, not a fact. The winding number is the more useful convention because it survives boolean operations in chapter 22.
Trap
A ray that passes exactly through a vertex double-counts. The standard fix — half-open scanline intervals — is unglamorous and must be stated explicitly or the reader will hit it in chapter 6 and blame the wrong code.
Tests
Winding number of a point inside a doubly-wound circle is 2; a pentagram's center is inside under nonzero and outside under even-odd.

In the GUI

Illustrator's Even-Odd / Non-Zero pair in the Attributes panel, and Inkscape's fill-rule toggle. It is also the reason "Reverse Path Direction" makes a hole appear or disappear.

06

Filling a Polygon

The classical scanline fill. Build the edge table, maintain the active edge list, step it down the raster, sort crossings, accumulate winding, emit spans. Hard edges only — this chapter is deliberately jaggy so that chapter 7 lands.

Builds
Edge table, active edge list, span emission, fill_path_aliased().
Insight
The whole algorithm is a sweep. Keeping the edges sorted incrementally rather than re-sorting each row is the difference between a demo and a renderer.
Trap
Horizontal edges contribute no crossings and must be dropped, not clamped. Every naive implementation draws spurious spikes at flat tops until it learns this.
Tests
Span list for an axis-aligned rectangle is exact; a triangle's span widths follow a known arithmetic sequence.
07

Analytic Antialiasing

The chapter the book exists for. Instead of sampling, walk each edge once and deposit two numbers into the cells it crosses: the signed area it contributes within that cell, and the signed height it carries into every cell to its right. Prefix-sum along each scanline. The result is exact under a box filter, sparse, and about a hundred and fifty lines.

Builds
Sparse cell accumulator, edge walker, scanline integrator. Replaces chapter 6's fill wholesale.
Insight
This is the algorithm inside FreeType's smooth rasterizer, stb_truetype, font-rs and Vello. The reader is not building a toy version of the real thing; they are building the real thing.
Trap
It is exact only for a box filter, and only for the shape considered alone. Neither caveat matters until chapter 9, where both do.
Tests
Diff every render against chapter 2's 64× supersampler with a tolerance of 1/255; a half-covered pixel reads 0.5 to six decimal places.
08

Curves

Quadratic and cubic Béziers. Evaluation, derivatives, splitting by de Casteljau, tight bounds via root finding. Then flattening: a flatness criterion, adaptive subdivision, and the observation that the tolerance must be measured after the transform or a scaled-up curve turns back into a polygon on screen.

Builds
Cubic, Quadratic, split_at, flatten(tolerance), SVG elliptical-arc conversion.
Insight
The rasterizer never sees a curve. Every curve in the book is a polyline by the time it reaches chapter 7, which is why chapter 7 could afford to only handle line segments.
Trap
The endpoint-to-center arc conversion in the SVG spec has out-of-range radius correction and two flag bits that select between four candidate arcs. It is worth a full worked example and its own test battery.
Tests
Split-and-rejoin reproduces the original curve; flattened length converges to the true arc length as tolerance shrinks; each of the four arc flag combinations hits a known point.

In the GUI

The pen tool's handles are the two interior control points, and the "smooth versus corner" anchor toggle is whether the incoming and outgoing handles are constrained collinear. Illustrator's Object → Path → Flatten is literally this chapter's function with the tolerance slider exposed.

Part III

Paint

Coverage says where. This part is entirely about what color, and about the surprisingly deep question of how a new color combines with what is already there.

09

Compositing

Premultiplied alpha and why it is not an optimization but a correctness requirement. Then source-over from first principles, then the full twelve Porter–Duff operators as a single parameterized equation, then the separable blend modes, and finally the four non-separable ones — hue, saturation, color and luminosity — which need a genuine little color-science digression.

Builds
Premultiplied pixel type, composite(op, src, dst), sixteen blend modes.
Insight
All twelve operators are one formula with two coefficient choices. Deriving that is far more satisfying than memorizing twelve cases.
Trap
Conflation. Two abutting shapes each covering half a pixel composite to 0.75, not 1.0, and a hairline seam appears along every shared edge. Every production renderer has this bug. This is the chapter where the abstraction leaks and the reader should be told plainly, along with what the mitigations cost.
Tests
Source-over with opaque source equals source; dst-over is src-over with arguments swapped; all sixteen blend modes reduce to normal when alpha is 1 and the mode is normal.

In the GUI

The layer blend-mode dropdown, top to bottom. Photoshop's Multiply, Screen and Overlay are the separable set; Hue, Saturation, Color and Luminosity at the bottom of the list are the four that need the color-science section.

10

Paint Servers and Gradients

Generalize "color" to "a function from device point to color". Solid, then linear, then radial in its two-circle focal form — which is a genuinely enjoyable quadratic solve with a real degenerate case — then conic. Stop tables, extend modes, interpolation space, and ordered dithering to defeat the banding that 8-bit output produces on any slow ramp.

Builds
Paint trait/interface, stop table with binary search, three gradient types, Bayer dither.
Insight
Once paint is a function of position, images, patterns and gradients stop being three features and become one.
Trap
When the focal point sits outside the end circle the radial gradient develops a cone of undefined region. The spec clamps it; naive implementations produce a black wedge.
Tests
Stop lookup at exact stop offsets; a linear gradient's value along its axis is the parameter itself; the radial degenerate case (r0 = r1) reduces to a linear gradient.
11

Images and Resampling

Read a PPM back in, then paint it through an arbitrary transform. The key move is inverting the matrix and walking destination pixels, not source ones. Nearest, bilinear, bicubic. Then minification, where every one of those filters fails, and mip pyramids as the standard answer.

Builds
PPM reader, ImagePaint with inverse mapping, three filters, mip chain, extend modes.
Insight
Magnification is reconstruction and minification is prefiltering. They are different problems and one filter cannot serve both.
Trap
The half-pixel offset. Texel centers are at 0.5, not 0, and getting this wrong shifts every resampled image by half a pixel — invisible in isolation, obvious in a comparison.
Tests
Identity transform is bit-exact under any filter; bilinear at a texel center returns that texel; a 2× mip level's average equals its parent's.

In the GUI

Photoshop's resample dropdown — Nearest Neighbor (hard edges), Bilinear, Bicubic Smoother, Bicubic Sharper — is this chapter's filter list with marketing names, and "Smoother versus Sharper" is the enlarge/reduce distinction the chapter derives.

12

Clipping, Masks and Groups

A rectangular clip first, then an arbitrary path clip — which is just another coverage buffer, multiplied in. Nesting clips is multiplication, so it composes for free. Then soft masks, then isolated groups, and the demonstration that group opacity is not the same as per-child opacity.

Builds
Clip stack, coverage multiplication, offscreen group buffers, push_group / pop_group_with_opacity.
Insight
Clipping needed no new machinery. That is the payoff for having made coverage a first-class value in chapter 2.
Trap
A group buffer must be allocated to the clip's bounding box, not the canvas, or a page of nested groups allocates gigabytes. Bounds propagation is the unglamorous half of this chapter.
Tests
Clip to full canvas is a no-op; nested clips commute; a group at opacity 1 is pixel-identical to drawing its children directly.

In the GUI

Illustrator's Make Clipping Mask, Figma's Clip content checkbox on a frame, and Photoshop's layer masks and clipping groups. The plate's right-hand side is exactly what happens when you group first and then drag the opacity slider.

Part IV

Strokes

The part everyone underestimates. Three chapters, no new rasterizer code, and more geometric subtlety than the whole of Part II.

13

Stroking is Filling

Convert a stroke into an outline path and hand it to chapter 7. Offset each segment by half the width on both sides, join the pieces, cap the ends. Miter, round and bevel joins; butt, round and square caps; the miter limit and what it means for the join to fall back.

Builds
stroke_to_path(path, width, caps, joins, miter_limit). No new rasterizer.
Insight
The renderer has exactly one drawing operation, and it is fill. Everything else is a path factory.
Trap
Zero-length subpaths, duplicate consecutive points, and 180° reversals all divide by zero in the naive normal calculation — and all three appear in real SVG files. A dot drawn with a round cap on a zero-length segment is a legitimate, specified construct.
Tests
A stroked horizontal segment with butt caps is exactly a rectangle; miter length at a known angle matches the closed form; the miter limit switches to bevel at exactly the documented threshold.

In the GUI

The three cap icons and three join icons in every stroke panel, in this order. Object → Path → Outline Stroke in Illustrator runs this function and replaces the path with its result — which is a good way for the reader to check their own output against a shipping implementation.

14

Offsetting Curves

The offset of a cubic Bézier is not a cubic Bézier — it is generally not a polynomial at all. So the chapter does what every real renderer does: subdivide until each piece is offsettable within tolerance, fit, and handle the cusps where the inner offset self-intersects.

Builds
offset_curve(cubic, d, tol), curvature evaluation, cusp detection and removal.
Insight
"Approximate within tolerance" is not a compromise here. It is the only available answer, and knowing that is worth a chapter.
Trap
Cheap strokers flatten first and offset the polyline, which is correct but produces enormous paths and visible faceting under later scaling. The chapter should show both and let the reader choose.
Tests
Offsetting a circular arc by d yields an arc of radius r ± d exactly; offset distance sampled at 100 points along the result stays within tolerance.
15

Dashes

Cutting a path into pieces at given arc lengths. Requires arc-length parameterization of curves, which Béziers do not offer in closed form, so: numeric integration or adaptive lookup tables. Then dash phase, phase carrying across segment boundaries but resetting at subpaths, and caps on every resulting fragment.

Builds
Arc-length table, split_at_length, dash(path, pattern, phase).
Insight
Dashing sits between path and stroker as a pure path-to-path transform, which keeps the stroker from growing a second job.
Trap
A dash pattern with an odd number of entries repeats doubled, and a pattern summing to zero must be ignored rather than looping forever. Both are specified behaviors that only a test will catch.
Tests
Dashes on a straight line land at exact multiples; total dash length plus gap length equals path length; phase equal to the pattern sum is a no-op.

In the GUI

The dash and gap boxes in the stroke panel, and the "align dashes to corners" toggle — which is a small redistribution pass on top of this chapter's function, worth mentioning as an exercise.

Part V

Type

Four chapters. The first three are achievable; the fourth is honest about being a reconnaissance mission into a field that is itself a book. Glyph data arrives as JSON so that no reader is blocked on binary parsing.

16

What a Glyph Is

Load glyphs.json and discover that a glyph is a path the reader can already fill. Quadratic contours, the on-curve flag, TrueType's implied on-curve midpoints between consecutive control points, opposite winding for counters, units-per-em and the scale to device pixels, and composite glyphs that reference other glyphs through a transform.

Builds
Font, Glyph, contour-to-path conversion, em-to-pixel scaling.
Insight
There is no type rendering code in this chapter. The counter in an o is a hole for exactly the same reason the pentagram's center was, back in chapter 5.
Trap
Fonts are y-up with the origin on the baseline; the canvas is y-down with the origin at the corner. The flip belongs in one place and the reader will put it in three.
Tests
Implied midpoints reconstruct a known contour; a composite glyph's bounding box matches the union of its transformed components.
Appendix
B: parsing real sfnt — the table directory, head, hhea/hmtx, cmap formats 4 and 12, loca, glyf. Optional, and CFF explicitly out of scope.
17

Rasterizing Type Well

Filling a glyph gives legible text. Making it look good at 11 pixels is a separate discipline: subpixel horizontal positioning, a glyph cache keyed by (glyph, size, subpixel offset), stem darkening to compensate for the thinning that correct gamma causes, and LCD subpixel rendering with the three-tap filter that keeps it from fringing.

Builds
Glyph cache and atlas, subpixel-positioned rasterization, LCD filter, stem darkening.
Insight
Text is the one place where physically correct blending looks wrong, and every operating system applies a fudge factor. Naming the fudge factor is more useful than pretending it isn't there.
Trap
LCD rendering assumes a specific physical subpixel order and breaks on rotated displays, OLED layouts and any composited transform. Explaining why the industry is quietly abandoning it is part of the chapter.
Tests
Cache hit returns an identical bitmap; the LCD filter's taps sum to 1; total ink is preserved under subpixel shift.

In the GUI

macOS's Use font smoothing when available, Windows' ClearType tuner, and the anti-alias mode dropdown (None / Sharp / Crisp / Strong / Smooth) in Photoshop's character panel. Hinting is deliberately skipped: it is dying with low-DPI screens.

18

Setting a Line of Text

Advance widths, the pen position, the baseline, ascent and descent and line gap. Kern pairs from the supplied JSON. Then breaking a paragraph at spaces, aligning it, and the realization that a text run is just a list of (glyph, position) pairs handed to chapter 16's path filler.

Builds
layout_run(), kern lookup, greedy line breaking, four alignments.
Insight
Layout and rendering are cleanly separable, and every serious text stack separates them. The reader now has both halves and can see the seam.
Trap
Rounding the pen position to whole pixels per glyph accumulates error and makes long lines drift; keeping it fractional is what chapter 17's subpixel cache was for.
Tests
Sum of advances equals the run width; a kerned pair is narrower than the unkerned sum by exactly the kern value; a line break never exceeds the measure.
19

Shaping — A Field Guide

The HarfBuzz-shaped chapter, scoped honestly. Itemize a string by script and direction; look up glyphs through cmap; apply a small hand-rolled subset of GSUB (ligature and contextual substitution) and GPOS (pair positioning and mark attachment); and track clusters so that cursor movement and selection stay correct when four characters became one glyph.

Goal
Shape one Latin ligature and one Arabic word correctly, from supplied rule tables — then understand precisely why the general case is somebody's life's work.
Builds
Itemizer, glyph buffer with cluster indices, a toy GSUB/GPOS interpreter over JSON rules.
Insight
The unit of text is not the character and not the glyph. It is the cluster, and every text bug you have ever seen in an app is somebody having forgotten that.
Trap
Bidi (UAX #9), grapheme clusters (UAX #29) and line breaking (UAX #14) are each larger than this chapter. They get a survey, a worked example, and a clear pointer outward — not an implementation.
Tests
f + i produces one glyph with a two-character cluster; an Arabic letter selects initial/medial/final/isolated by neighbor; cursor positions map back to character offsets.

In the GUI

InDesign's OpenType feature menu — Standard Ligatures, Discretionary Ligatures, Contextual Alternates, Oldstyle Figures — is a checkbox per GSUB feature tag. CSS exposes the identical set through font-feature-settings.

Part VI

The Whole Machine

Nothing new is invented here. Two chapters that connect what the reader built to art somebody else made — which is the moment the Ray Tracer Challenge's OBJ chapter earns its place, and this book's equivalent should be even more dramatic.

20

Rendering SVG

The capstone. Parse the document with the reader's XML library of choice, then hand-write the parts that matter: the d attribute's mini-language, viewBox and preserveAspectRatio, the transform attribute grammar, presentation attributes and inheritance, referenced gradients and clip paths. Every drawing feature it needs already exists.

Builds
Path-data tokenizer, transform parser, style cascade, a render tree walker.
Insight
The reader is not writing a renderer for the tiger. The tiger renders because the previous nineteen chapters were built in the right order — which is the whole argument of the book, made visible in one image.
Trap
The path grammar permits implicit repeated commands, omitted separators, exponent notation, and a flags packed without delimiters (a1 1 0 0110 0 is legal). A tokenizer written from intuition parses 95% of files and fails on the tiger.
Tests
A corpus of path-data strings with known point sequences, including every abbreviation form; the W3C test suite as a stretch goal.
21

Making It Fast

Now that it is correct: measure, then fix. Tile the canvas, classify tiles as empty, partial or solid, and run the expensive path only on the partial ones. Paint by spans instead of pixels. Keep the coverage accumulator sparse. Think in terms of data layout and batching before reaching for anything platform-specific.

Builds
Tile classifier, span painter, a benchmark harness, and a profile of the tiger before and after.
Insight
The tile classification is the single biggest win and it is about thirty lines. Ordering the optimizations by payoff-per-line is the actual lesson.
Trap
Every optimization here changes rounding somewhere. The golden-image suite from chapter 0 is what makes this chapter safe to attempt at all.
Tests
The full golden-image corpus must be pixel-identical before and after every change in this chapter.

§ 21.4  Four pixels at a time

A short, self-contained section on SIMD, built around one measurable win. After tiling, the hottest loop in the renderer is the span compositor: for every pixel in a run, blend a premultiplied source over the destination. It is four independent byte multiplies per pixel with no branches and no dependency between iterations — which is the exact shape a vector unit wants.

// scalar — one pixel, four channels, per iteration
for x in span:
    ia = 255 - src[x].a
    for ch in [r, g, b, a]:
        dst[x][ch] = src[x][ch] + mul255(dst[x][ch], ia)

// 4-wide — one register holds four pixels of one channel
for x in span step 4:
    s  = load_4px(src, x)          // 16 bytes
    d  = load_4px(dst, x)
    ia = splat_alpha(s)          // each pixel's a, broadcast to its lanes
    d  = mul255_16(d, ia)         // 16 byte multiplies, one instruction
    store_4px(dst, x, add_sat(s, d))

Two honest caveats belong in the text. First, mul255 is not a divide — the standard (v*a + 128); (v + v>>8) >> 8 approximation must be used identically in both versions, or the golden-image suite will light up over a rounding difference nobody can see. That is precisely what the suite is for. Second, expect around 3× on this loop and far less than that on the whole render, because Amdahl has not gone anywhere. The section ends by re-profiling to show how much of the win survived.

The book stays language-agnostic here, so the pseudocode above maps onto whatever portable vector API the reader's language offers — Rust's std::simd, C#'s System.Numerics.Vector<T>, Java's Vector API, NumPy for Python. If there is none, the fallback is the more valuable lesson anyway: write the loop so the compiler can vectorize it. Contiguous data, a fixed trip count, no aliasing between source and destination, no branches inside. Two larger wins need no vector code at all — detecting fully opaque spans and replacing the blend with a copy, and hoisting solid-color paint out of the per-pixel path entirely.

22

Boolean Path Operations

This book's answer to the CSG chapter. Find every intersection between two paths, split both at those points, classify each resulting segment as inside or outside the other path, and stitch the survivors back into contours. A sweep-line makes the intersection step tractable.

Builds
Segment intersection, a Bentley–Ottmann-style sweep, segment classification, contour reassembly.
Insight
The winding number from chapter 5 is what makes classification a lookup rather than a case analysis. This chapter is the reward for having chosen the right convention early.
Trap
Numerical robustness is the entire difficulty. Near-coincident edges, tangential touches and shared endpoints all produce topologically impossible results with naive floating point. The chapter should be candid that this is the hardest code in the book.
Tests
Union of a shape with itself is itself; A minus A is empty; area of the union plus area of the intersection equals the sum of the areas.

In the GUI

Illustrator's Pathfinder panel and Figma's boolean row, icon for icon: Unite, Subtract, Intersect, Exclude. Same four operations, same order, in every vector editor ever shipped.

23

Distance Fields

Store the distance to the nearest edge instead of the coverage of the pixel, and a startling number of the previous twenty-two chapters become one-liners. This chapter is a second, complete answer to the question the book has been asking, and the reader implements all of it.

Exercises
  1. Exact fields for primitives. Segment, circle, box, rounded box. Then the polygon: minimum distance over all edges for the magnitude, winding number from chapter 5 for the sign.
  2. Fields for curves. The quadratic Bézier reduces to a cubic root solve; the cubic needs Newton iteration from a subdivision-derived seed. Both converge fast and both are testable against brute force.
  3. Render it. coverage = clamp(0.5 − d, 0, 1). Then diff against chapter 7's exact rasterizer and measure where it is wrong — the error is near zero along straight edges and significant at sharp corners, which is the whole story of this technique in one experiment.
  4. Get chapters 13, 14 and 22 for free. Offset is d − r. Stroke is abs(d) − w/2. Union is min, intersection is max, difference is max(a, −b). Re-render the plates from those chapters this way and compare both the code length and the output.
  5. Then do something paths cannot. Replace min with a polynomial smooth minimum and the union grows a fillet whose radius you control. There is no path expression for that shape.
  6. Distance transform. Convert chapter 2's coverage buffer into a field with a two-pass exact Euclidean transform, so that any bitmap — including a scanned one — becomes a field.
  7. Glyph atlases. Bake chapter 16's outlines into a small SDF texture, render it at twenty times the baked resolution, and watch the corners round off. Then fix them by implementing the median-of-three multi-channel trick, and watch them come back.
Insight
Rasterization was a choice, not a law. Meeting a second complete representation — with a different set of cheap operations and a different set of impossible ones — is the best available last lesson about the first one.
Trap
min(a, b) is not the signed distance field of the union. It is correct outside the shape and wrong inside it, so the error is invisible until the reader offsets or strokes the result, at which point it is very visible. Distance fields compose approximately; paths compose exactly. That trade is the chapter's real content.
Tests
Sampled distances match a brute-force nearest-point search over 10,000 random points; abs(d) − w/2 agrees with chapter 13's stroker to within a tolerance; the distance transform matches an O(n²) reference exactly.

In the GUI

Photoshop's layer effects — Stroke, Outer Glow, Drop Shadow, Spread — are thresholds on a distance field, which is why they are instant and why their corners look the way they do. Illustrator's Offset Path is the exact version and is noticeably slower.

24

Doing It the GPU's Way

A book that only writes PPM files cannot run a shader — but it can implement every algorithm the GPU uses, as ordinary code that obeys the GPU's constraints. That is the exercise, and the constraints are the lesson: no unbounded per-pixel work, no sorting, no shared mutable state between work items, fixed-size tiles. Written under that discipline, these algorithms run perfectly well on a CPU — they simply look nothing like the ones in Part II.

Exercises
  1. Curves without flattening (Loop–Blinn). Give a quadratic's three control points the texture coordinates (0,0), (½,0) and (1,1), rasterize the control triangle with chapter 6's scanline code, and interpolate. A pixel is inside the curve exactly when u² − v < 0. Render a glyph from chapter 16 this way, with the flattener switched off entirely. TrueType outlines are quadratic, so this covers every glyph the book ships; the cubic classification into serpentines, loops and cusps is stated and left as an extension.
  2. Filling without sorting (stencil-and-cover). Pick any anchor point. For every edge, add ±1 to an integer buffer over the triangle (anchor, pi, pi+1), signed by the edge's direction. Then fill the bounding box, keeping pixels whose count is nonzero. No active edge list, no crossing sort, no sequential dependency — and the buffer that falls out is literally the winding number from chapter 5. Count the operations against chapter 7 and find out what the parallelism costs.
  3. The compute pipeline. Restructure chapter 21's tiler into four stages that each read one array and write another, sharing nothing: flatten to segments, bin segments into tiles, build a per-tile command list, execute each tile into a 16×16 block. Then the test that proves the point — execute the tiles in shuffled order and assert the output is bit-identical. If it is, the renderer would parallelize; if it is not, the reader has found their hidden shared state.
  4. Paint as a shader. Rewrite Part III's paint servers as pure functions of position with no access to neighboring pixels and no branching on state. Most of them already are, which is the point: the reader has been writing GPU-shaped code since chapter 10 without being told.
  5. Sampling instead of area. Implement 4× and 16× rotated-grid multisampling and compare it against chapter 7's analytic coverage on a near-horizontal edge. This is the antialiasing the hardware actually offers, and seeing exactly how it falls short explains why every GPU 2D library eventually writes its own rasterizer anyway.
Insight
The GPU is not faster because it is magic. It is faster because it refuses to run algorithms with sequential dependencies, and these are the algorithms that survive that refusal. Chapter 7 is smarter; chapter 24 is dumber and scales.
Trap
Per-tile independence dies the moment a tile needs to read the destination — which is exactly what chapter 9's blend modes and chapter 12's groups do. Every real GPU 2D renderer has an unhappy path for this, and the reader should build the unhappy path once so they know it is there.
Tests
Loop–Blinn output matches the flattened rasterizer within tolerance; the stencil buffer equals winding_at() from chapter 5 at every pixel; shuffled tile execution is bit-identical; MSAA converges towards analytic coverage as sample count rises.
Reading
Now that it is all built: Loop & Blinn on resolution-independent curves, Nehab & Hoppe on random-access vector textures, Levien on stroke expansion, and the Vello architecture notes — which read very differently once the reader has implemented three of the ideas in them.
Bonus B

The Raster Editor Detour

You mentioned MSPaint, and it is a genuinely different paradigm — editing pixels directly rather than compiling geometry into them. It does not belong on the through-line, but it makes an excellent optional part that can be read any time after chapter 3.

B1–B4

Brushes, Buckets and Dithering

Four short chapters. Stamping: a brush is a coverage kernel deposited along an interpolated pointer path, and spacing it by distance rather than by event is what stops it looking beaded. Flood fill: scanline fill, connectivity, tolerance, and the halo that antialiasing leaves behind — which is why every bucket tool has a tolerance slider and an anti-alias checkbox that fight each other. Quantization: median cut, ordered dithering, Floyd–Steinberg error diffusion, and writing an indexed-color file. Selection and undo: the marquee as a coverage mask (chapter 12 again), floating layers, and a command stack.

Insight
A raster editor's tools are the same coverage machinery with the geometry supplied by a human hand instead of a path.
Trap
Naive flood fill recurses per pixel and blows the stack on a full-canvas fill. The scanline variant is both faster and safe, and is the only version worth teaching.

In the GUI

Brush spacing, hardness and flow; the paint bucket's Tolerance, Contiguous and Anti-alias controls; Image → Mode → Indexed Color with its dither dropdown; the magic wand, which is a flood fill that returns a mask instead of painting one.

Testing

Chapter 0, and the thing that is genuinely harder than in the Ray Tracer Challenge

Jamis's Gherkin scenarios work because ray–sphere intersection has an exact scalar answer you can write in a feature file. Most of this book's output does not. A test that asserts an exact 400×400 image is unwritable, unreadable, and breaks on every legitimate refactor.

So the tests come in three tiers, and every chapter labels which tier its tests belong to. Chapter 1 opens with the notation and the tolerance rule, and uses all three tiers before it ends, so the reader has met each kind before chapter 2. Being explicit about this up front is what stops the reader from concluding halfway through that the tests are unreliable. Whether the tiers also deserve a chapter 0 of their own is still open.

TierFormApplies to
ExactGherkin, as in RTC Transfer functions, matrices, curve splitting, arc conversion, winding numbers, blend equations, dash arithmetic, kern sums. Roughly half the book, and it reads exactly like the original.
CoverageScalar, with tolerance Then the coverage at 3,4 is 0.5 ± 0.001 — asserted on hand-computable geometry: half-planes through pixel centers, axis-aligned rectangles, triangles with known area. This is how the rasterizer itself gets tested without asserting whole images.
GoldenImage diff, with a budget Full renders compared against a stored PPM: maximum per-channel delta, and a count of pixels differing at all. Chapter 2's brute-force supersampler is the reference for anything the reader can't hand-compute. The book ships the corpus.

Still open

Decisions I'd want settled before drafting

  1. 01

    Is Part V load-bearing or a bonus?

    As outlined, the tiger is the finale and type is Part V — so a reader could stop at chapter 15 and still have a renderer. If instead the book's destination were "set a paragraph of Arabic correctly", the whole structure reorients around the text stack and grows by perhaps a third. The current outline assumes not.

  2. 02

    Linear-light or encoded-space blending?

    Settled. Correct is linear. Matching what readers see in Figma and Chrome is encoded. The book blends in linear throughout: the canvas stores light from chapter 1 and only the PPM writer encodes. Chapter 1 ships mix(a, b, t) with a global linear-blending switch, pinned both ways in Gherkin, and a side-by-side plate, because the reader will compare their output to a browser and needs to know why it differs.

  3. 03

    How much of the JSON glyph format do you specify up front?

    One schema covering chapters 16–19 is cleaner to document but front-loads GSUB concepts into chapter 16. Growing the format per chapter matches the pedagogy but means the reader edits their loader three times. I lean towards one schema with clearly optional sections.

  4. 04

    Does chapter 22 make the cut?

    Boolean operations are the most-requested feature of any vector renderer and the hardest code in the book by a wide margin, mostly for robustness reasons that are unrewarding to teach. It may serve the reader better as a well-written appendix that is honest about the difficulty than as a chapter they are expected to complete.

  5. 05

    Fixed point or floating point?

    RTC is floats throughout and never suffers for it. I'd keep floats for clarity and confine the 24.8 fixed-point discussion to chapter 21, where it belongs as an optimization rather than a foundation.
