Chapter Fourteen

Offsetting Curves

Stroke a curve with the stroker you have. The curve gets flattened on the way in, chapter 13 sees a polyline of twenty-two short segments, and what comes back is twenty-two rectangles and twenty-one round joins: 172 points, to describe a shape whose true boundary is two smooth curves and two flat ends. It looks right. It's also the reason Illustrator's Offset Path gives you back curves and your renderer gives you back gravel. This chapter builds the real thing: the curve that runs parallel to a Bézier at a fixed distance. There's a catch, and it's not a small one. That curve isn't a Bézier. It isn't a polynomial of any degree. Nobody can write it down, so everybody approximates it and rounds the fact off, and knowing exactly how they do that is worth a chapter.

Why it can't be exact is easy to see once you write it out. The offset of a curve p(t) at distance d is p(t) + d·n(t), where n is the unit normal. The normal is the tangent turned a quarter turn and divided by its length, and that length is a square root. A cubic's tangent is a quadratic; its length is the square root of a quartic; and no polynomial has a square root of a quartic in it. So the offset is a genuinely different kind of curve, and a cubic can only ever come close to it. "Close within a tolerance" is not a compromise here. It's the only answer there is.

§ 14.1 The offset point

One point at a time, though, the offset is easy. At any t you have the point from chapter 8's point_at and the tangent from derivative. Normalize the tangent, turn it a quarter turn toward +y, and you have the unit normal. Which side is that? On the y-down canvas, turning a rightward tangent toward +y points it down, which is the right-hand side of someone walking along the curve on screen, and it's the same +h side chapter 13's rectangles used. Positive d is the right of travel; negative is the left. The scenarios pin both, on a straight cubic where you can check the answer by hand and on a cubic that bottoms out at (2, 3), where d = 1 lands at (2, 4) and d = −1 at (2, 2).

One thing will bite you the first time a real file arrives. Illustrator, when a handle isn't being used, drops it exactly on its anchor, and a cubic whose first control point equals its first anchor has a derivative of zero at t = 0. You can't normalize a zero. The curve still leaves its start in a perfectly definite direction; it does so at zero speed. So when the derivative vanishes at the parameter you asked for, take the direction a hair further into the curve instead: t + 0.0001 at the start, t − 0.0001 at the end. The scenario stalls a cubic on its first anchor and checks that the offset point still comes out where the geometry says.

§ 14.2 Curvature, and where the offset stalls

Take the derivative of the offset and something useful falls out. The point moves at the curve's speed. The normal turns as the curve bends, and how fast it turns is, by definition, the curvature. Put the two together and the offset at distance d moves at (1 − κd) times the speed of the curve, where κ is the signed curvature. That one factor explains everything that goes wrong in this chapter. On the outside of a bend κd is negative and the offset runs faster than the curve, which is harmless. On the inside it's positive and the offset slows down. When d reaches 1/κ, which is the radius of the circle hugging the curve at that point, the offset stops dead. Beyond it, the factor goes negative and the offset runs backward: it doubles back through itself and draws a little loop, a swallowtail, pointing the wrong way. You'll see one in every drawing program that offsets a tight bend, and Plate 14 has three.

You need curvature as a number, then. For a parametric curve it's κ = cross(v, a) / |v|³, with v the first derivative and a the second. The second derivative of a Bézier is the same trick as the first, one degree further down: a quadratic's second derivative is the constant 2·(P0 − 2P1 + P2), and a cubic's is the straight-line blend 6·((1 − t)·(P0 − 2P1 + P2) + t·(P1 − 2P2 + P3)). The sign of κ follows chapter 4's cross: positive where the curve turns clockwise on screen. That's the same side positive d points to, which is why the stall condition reads κd = 1 and not −1: positive curvature and positive d are both the clockwise side, so their product is positive on the inside of a turn.

The parameters where the offset stalls are the cusps, and you'll need them, because no single smooth curve can be fitted through a point where the direction flips. Finding them is a root problem: where does 1 − κ(t)·d change sign? There's no closed form for that either, so cusps(c, d) does the honest thing: evaluate it at 64 evenly spaced parameters, and wherever two neighbours disagree in sign, bisect between them forty times. Two cusps closer together than 1/64 of the parameter range can hide between samples; a fold that small is narrower than anything you'll draw. The parabola in the scenarios has curvature −1 at its vertex, so the offset on its inside (the −d side, since the parabola turns counterclockwise) stalls the moment d passes 1, and at d = −2 the two cusps sit where the curvature is exactly −1/2.

§ 14.3 Fitting one cubic, and admitting the miss

Now to approximate. The plan is the one every real renderer uses: fit a cubic to a piece of the offset, measure how far off it is, and if it's too far, cut the piece in half and fit each half. The fit itself is small. You know where the offset starts and ends (two offset points) and which way it's heading at each end (the curve's own tangents, because a parallel curve has parallel tangents). That fixes the cubic's two anchors and the directions of its two handles. What's left is how long each handle is, two unknowns, and one more fact pins them: the fitted cubic should pass through the offset point at t = 0.5 when its own t is 0.5. A cubic's midpoint is (P0 + 3P1 + 3P2 + P3)/8. Write P1 = P0 + a·T0 and P2 = P3 − b·T1, set the midpoint equal to the target M, and it collapses to two equations in two unknowns:

fit_offset(c, d):
  P0 ← offset_point(c, 0, d);  P3 ← offset_point(c, 1, d)
  T0 ← tangent_at(c, 0);       T1 ← tangent_at(c, 1)
  M  ← offset_point(c, 0.5, d)
  R  ← (8M − 4P0 − 4P3) / 3          // so that a·T0 − b·T1 = R
  if |cross(T0, T1)| < 1e-9:          // parallel tangents: no unique answer
    a ← b ← |P3 − P0| / 3
  else:
    a ← cross(R, T1) / cross(T0, T1)
    b ← cross(R, T0) / cross(T0, T1)
  return cubic(P0, P0 + a·T0, P3 − b·T1, P3)

Cross both sides of a·T0 − b·T1 = R with T1 and the b term vanishes; cross with T0 and the a term does. When the two tangents are parallel, the equation has no unique solution, and it doesn't deserve one: the piece is either straight, in which case a third of the chord for each handle is exactly right, or it's a U-turn, in which case no single cubic is going to fit and the error check below will say so loudly. The scenarios pin both: the straight line's fit is exact, and the U-turn's fit misses by 2.5.

How to measure the miss is a choice. The cheap way is at matched parameters: at t = 0, 1/16, …, 1, compare the fitted point to the true offset point and take the worst. That's offset_error. It's cheap, it's deterministic, and it's a bound: the fitted point can't be further from the offset curve than it is from one particular point on it. It's also pessimistic, because the two curves don't run at the same speed, so it flags pieces that are geometrically fine and makes the subdivision below cut a little more than it strictly needs to. The honest measure is the distance from a point to the curve, and you'll want that anyway. distance_to_curve(c, p) has no closed form either (it's a quintic for a cubic), so it's sampling again: the nearest of 65 samples at t = i/64, then 32 rounds of ternary search on the bracket from the sample before it to the sample after it (clamped to 0 and 1 at the ends), which narrows the bracket to a millionth. The scenarios pin it on a straight line, where the answer is a perpendicular; past the end of a line, where it's the distance to the endpoint; and below a parabola's vertex, where the nearest point is not the vertex, and the answer happens to be √3.

§ 14.4 The offset curve

Put the pieces together. Split the curve at its cusps, so no piece contains a flip. Then fit each piece; if the fit is within tolerance, keep it; if not, split the piece at t = 0.5 and do both halves. Chapter 8's split_at does the cutting, and the piece between two arbitrary parameters is two cuts, which sub_curve(c, t0, t1) wraps up. Cap the recursion at sixteen halvings so a piece that will never converge (there's always one) stops subdividing and takes what it has.

offset_curve(c, d, tolerance):
  ts ← [0] + cusps(c, d) + [1]
  pieces ← []
  for each consecutive (t0, t1) in ts:
    offset_into(sub_curve(c, t0, t1), d, tolerance, pieces, depth 0)
  return pieces

offset_into(c, d, tolerance, pieces, depth):
  fitted ← fit_offset(c, d)
  if offset_error(c, d, fitted) ≤ tolerance or depth = 16:  append fitted
  else:  offset_into(each half of split_at(c, 0.5), d, tolerance, pieces, depth + 1)

The result is a list of cubics, in order, that chain end to end from the first offset point to the last. A quarter circle offset outward needs one piece at a tolerance of 0.1 and four at 0.01; a U-turn needs four even at a loose tolerance, because that first fit was hopeless. And now the honest check: walk 100 points along the result and ask distance_to_curve how far each one is from the original. On the outside of a bend, every one is |d| away to within the tolerance, which is the property the whole construction exists to deliver, and offset_distance_error reports the worst miss. The hundred points are spread by piece: u = i/99 times the number of pieces, on piece floor(u) at parameter u − floor(u), so the scenario can pin its value on a fold as well as bound it.

On the inside of a tight bend it isn't, and this is the point of §14.2 arriving in the scenarios. Offset the parabola by −2, past its vertex radius of 1, and the pieces still chain perfectly: through the first cusp, back to (2, 0) directly above the vertex, out through the second cusp. But (2, 0) is only √3 from the curve, not 2, because the parabola's arms curve in toward it. The offset is exactly right, point by point, and it's still closer than d: that's what a fold is. It is not a bug in the fit. What you do about it depends on what the offset is for, and the next section does the case that matters most.

§ 14.5 Stroking a curve: one outline

A stroke is the region within h = width/2 of the curve, and its boundary is the offset at +h, the offset at −h, and two caps. So build exactly that as one closed subpath: the right offset, flattened, forward; the end cap's points; the left offset, flattened, backward; the start cap's points. The caps are chapter 13's shapes, walked as points instead of emitted as separate pieces. Fill it nonzero. It's worth writing the whole thing down because of how short it is:

stroke_curve_to_path(c, width, cap, tolerance):
  h ← width / 2;  pts ← []
  for piece in offset_curve(c, +h, tolerance):  pts += flatten(piece, tolerance)
  pts += cap points at point_at(c, 1), heading tangent_at(c, 1)
  for piece in offset_curve(c, −h, tolerance), last to first:  pts += flatten(piece, tolerance) reversed
  pts += cap points at point_at(c, 0), heading −tangent_at(c, 0)
  drop consecutive duplicates, and the last point if it equals the first
  return one closed subpath through pts        // fill it nonzero

Figures 14.3 and 14.4 draw each outline over its fill the way Plate 13 did: every edge with chapter 3's line_wu, its endpoints rounded to the nearest pixel, halves up, the same rule chapter 1's byte conversion uses: floor(v + 0.5), which takes 42.5 to 43 and −17.5 to −17. This is the first time the book rounds a negative half, and it's the first time the rule matters at all: the hairpin is symmetric and two of its outline points land exactly on a half, so a language whose rounding takes halves to even, or away from zero, draws a different picture there and misses the reference by a pixel column. The scenario pins both points and both roundings.

Compare it with the way you were stroking curves until this morning. flatten_then_stroke flattens the curve at the same tolerance and hands the polyline to chapter 13 with round joins. Both are within a pixel of the truth, and the fills agree to within a fraction of a pixel of coverage anywhere. Then count the points. For the hairpin in the scenarios, chapter 13's version is 43 subpaths and 172 points; the outline is one subpath and 58. Figure 14.3 draws both outlines over their fills, and the difference in what you're holding is the entire picture.

The trap

Flattening first is not wrong. Its picture is right, and it's what this book's renderer does for the tiger, because a flattened path is what the fill wants anyway. The trap is treating the polyline outline as the geometry. It's twenty-two rectangles and twenty-one wedges glued together at the tolerance you happened to flatten at. Scale it up later and the facets show. Export it and the file is enormous. Offset it again, as a font engine or a CNC toolpath does, and the wedges multiply. The offset curve is the thing to keep when the outline has to survive as a shape: Illustrator's Offset Path, a synthesized bold, a pocket-milling contour. When the outline only has to become pixels, flatten and don't feel bad about it. The chapter builds both so you can choose, and the scenario pins that they draw the same thing.

About that loop. The hairpin is cubic((35, 140), (65, −30), (95, −30), (125, 140)) in a 160-pixel panel; its tightest radius is about 8, and the stroke is 60 wide, so the inner offset at +30 stalls and doubles back: the outline has a swallowtail in it and crosses itself. What does the fill do with that? The same thing it did with chapter 5's pentagram. Under the nonzero rule the loop is wound, so it's inside; under even-odd it's wound twice and becomes a hole. Figure 14.4 fills one outline under both rules. The hole is where the pen was, sweeping through the inside of the bend, so nonzero is the rule that tells the truth, and the scenario pins it against the honest measure: a point inside the loop is closer than h to the curve and inside under nonzero and outside under even-odd, a point beyond the loop is further than h and outside under both. No cusp trimming, no intersection finding: split at the cusps so every piece fits, leave the fold in the outline, and let the fill rule remove it.

The scenarios also pin what an outline is: one closed subpath, whose first point is the right offset of the curve's start and whose last point is the left offset of it, with the round cap adding thirty points and the square cap four. One curve, one outline. A path of several curves joined end to end would take chapter 13's joins between the outlines of its pieces, and the book's paths are polylines, so it doesn't build that; the tiger flattens.

In the GUI

Illustrator's Object → Path → Offset Path is offset_curve with a dialog: Offset is d, Joins picks what happens at the corners between curves, and the Miter limit field is chapter 13's. Inkscape's Path → Dynamic Offset is the same function with a handle you drag, and you can watch the swallowtails appear as you cross the radius.

§ 14.6 Putting it together

The plate is the whole chapter in one picture: an arch, its offsets at 15, 30, 45 and 60 on both sides, and every cusp marked. The arch's tightest radius is about 26. On the outside the offsets nest like contour lines, each a smooth parallel of the last. On the inside, 15 clears the radius and is fine; 30, 45 and 60 don't, and each folds into a swallowtail whose two tips are exactly where cusps said they'd be. Every line is an offset curve flattened and stroked hairline-thin with chapter 13, and the dots are chapter 5's circles.

offsets_plate():
  c ← canvas(320, 270), filled with paper
  curve ← arch()                                     // cubic (60,250) (130,5) (190,5) (260,250)
  for k, d in (15, 30, 45, 60):  hairline(c, offset_path(curve, −d, 0.1), COOL[k])
  for k, d in (15, 30, 45, 60):  hairline(c, offset_path(curve, +d, 0.1), WARM[k])
  hairline(c, flatten(curve, 0.1), white, width 2)
  for d in (15, 30, 45, 60):
    for t in cusps(curve, d):  fill circle_path(offset_point(curve, t, d), 2.5, 24) magenta
  return c

hairline(c, p, color, width 1.5):
  paint fill_path(stroke_to_path(p, width, "butt", "round", 4), "nonzero") through color

plate_14():
  return magnify(offsets_plate(), 2)
