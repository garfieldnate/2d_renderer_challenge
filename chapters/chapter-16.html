Chapter Sixteen

What a Glyph Is

Here is a font. Not a file format with a table directory and four kinds of checksum, a JSON file: one number called units_per_em, a table from characters to names, and for every name a list of loops of points, each point flagged on or off. Draw the letter a. That's the whole chapter, and by the end of it you'll have noticed that it contained no type rendering code at all. A glyph is a path. It was drawn by somebody who knew what they were doing, in a coordinate system that's upside down from yours, with a shorthand for its curves that you have to expand, and sometimes it's assembled out of other glyphs. But it's a path, and you have been filling paths since chapter 7. The counter in the a is a hole for exactly the reason the pentagram's centre was in chapter 5.

Part V is four chapters on type. This one is the shape of a letter. The next is what it takes to make that shape look good at eleven pixels, which is a separate discipline with its own fudge factors. Then a line of letters, then the honest reconnaissance of what happens when the letters are Arabic. All of it stands on the renderer you have; nothing in it draws a pixel a new way.

§ 16.1 The font file

Real fonts are binary sfnt files, and parsing one is a detour: a table directory, a dozen tables with names like hhea and loca, three encodings of the character map, and a glyph format with flag bytes and delta-coded coordinates. It's fine work and it's Appendix B. The book hands you the result instead: reference/chapter-16/roboto.json is Roboto Regular, an Apache-licensed typeface, cut down to the 177 glyphs the type chapters use and written out as JSON. Every language reads JSON. Here's the schema.

The glyph file

units_per_em, ascender, descender, line_gap: numbers in font units. cmap: codepoint (as a string key) to glyph name. glyphs: name to {advance, contours, components}, where contours is a list of loops, each a list of [x, y, on] points in font units with y up, and components is a list of {glyph, transform} with the transform six numbers [a, b, c, d, dx, dy]. Two sections are optional and arrive later: kern, a list of [left, right, value], for chapter 18; and ligatures, a list of [[parts...], result], for chapter 19. One loader, written once.

An em is the design grid: every coordinate in the file is a whole number on a grid units_per_em across, and Roboto's is 2048. A glyph's advance is how far the pen moves after drawing it, on the same grid. The ascender and descender are how far the font reaches above and below the baseline, and the line gap is extra space between lines; chapter 18 uses all three. load_font reads the file; glyph_name looks a character up in the cmap and answers .notdef, the box every font draws for a character it doesn't have, when the character isn't there. The scenarios pin the file's numbers, so you'll know your loader read what the book's did.

§ 16.2 Contours, and the points that aren't there

A TrueType contour is a closed loop of points, and each point is flagged on-curve or off-curve. On-curve points are on the outline. Off-curve points are the control points of quadratic Béziers: an on-curve point, an off-curve point, the next on-curve point make one of chapter 8's quadratics. Two on-curve points in a row are a straight edge. So far that's a path with a curve command, and you could write it out as one. The shorthand is what happens with two off-curve points in a row. The format doesn't allow a quadratic to have two control points, so it means something else: there's an on-curve point between them, exactly at their midpoint, and the file didn't bother to store it. A smooth curve through a dozen control points is stored as a dozen off-curve points and nothing else, and the on-curve points are all implied. The dot on Roboto's i is nine points, eight of them off-curve; the loop wraps, so the last and first points count as consecutive too, and a loop may start on an off-curve point, in which case the first thing you do is rotate it round to one that's on.

implied_points does the expansion: walk the loop, and after every off-curve point that's followed by another off-curve point, insert the midpoint flagged on; then rotate the result to start on an on-curve point. contour_curves turns that into quadratics, and it treats a straight edge as a quadratic too, with its control point at the midpoint of the edge, which is the same line exactly (a Bézier with its control point on the chord is the chord). It costs nothing and it means every piece of every contour is the same shape, which the next section leans on. The scenarios build tiny loops by hand, where you can check the midpoints on paper, then check the i against the file.

§ 16.3 Composites

Open eacute in the file and there are no contours at all. There's a list of two components: the glyph e through an identity transform, and the glyph acute moved 340 units to the right. Half of Roboto is like this, because half of any Latin font is accented letters, and drawing the accent once is how a type designer stays sane. A component's transform is six numbers, and TrueType applies them as x' = a·x + c·y + dx, y' = b·x + d·y + dy, which is a matrix3 with the letters in a slightly surprising order: component_matrix builds it. glyph_outline is then the same thing for every glyph: its own contours as quadratics, followed by each component's outline taken through its matrix, recursively, since a component can itself be a composite. In font units, y up; the flip hasn't happened yet.

Two things need bounds: chapter 17's bitmaps, and the check that a composite came out where its parts went. glyph_bounds is the tight box of the outline, from chapter 8's curve_bounds on every quadratic, not the box of the control points, which pokes out past the curves. A composite's bounds are the union of its transformed components' bounds, and the scenario checks that for eacute: the e's box grown upward to the top of the accent. An empty glyph (space) has bounds (0, 0, 0, 0).

§ 16.4 The flip, in one place

The trap

Fonts are y-up with the origin on the baseline, at the left of the glyph. Your canvas is y-down with the origin at the top-left corner. Something has to turn the glyph over, and it has to happen exactly once. The failure isn't forgetting it: a forgotten flip draws every letter upside down, hanging below the baseline, and you'll notice (Figure 16.4 is what it looks like). The failure is doing it in three places, because it's a one-liner and the loader, the path builder and the renderer each seemed like the natural home, and then two of them cancel and the third is wrong for composites only. Put it in text_matrix and nowhere else. Every other function in this chapter and the next three works in font units, y up, and takes a matrix when it needs to leave them.

text_matrix(font, size, x, y) is chapter 4 doing what chapter 4 does: scale by size / units_per_em with the y scale negated, then translate so the glyph's origin lands on the baseline at (x, y). Since the scale is the same in both axes, chapter 8's rule about flattening after the transform is the whole reason glyph_outline stays in font units: glyph_path takes every quadratic through the matrix, flattens it in device pixels at the tolerance you give it, and closes each contour into a subpath. The same outline at 12 pixels and at 96 gets the number of segments each size deserves.

Fill the path nonzero, and the winding takes care of the rest. TrueType draws outer contours clockwise in its own y-up coordinates and counters the other way, so the counter of an o winds against its outside, and under the nonzero rule that's a hole, the same hole chapter 5 found in the pentagram. The flip turns both contours over, which swaps clockwise and counterclockwise, and the scenario pins the sign of each contour's polygon_area on both sides of it. Then the check that says the counter really is a hole: ink of the filled o is the outer contour's area minus the inner's, to four decimal places, because chapter 7's fill is exact and holes are only areas with the opposite sign.

In the GUI

Illustrator's Type → Create Outlines is glyph_path with the scale baked in: the text stops being text and becomes ordinary paths, and the anchor points it shows you are the on-curve points you drew in this chapter. Turn a word into outlines and count the anchors on an o; you'll find the implied ones.

§ 16.5 Putting it together

The plate is the letter a, filled, with the file's data drawn over it: the control polygon as a hairline, on-curve points as filled squares, off-curve points as hollow circles, and the implied on-curve points as smaller squares. Two contours, wound in opposite directions, and every curve a quadratic. Look at how few points it takes.

glyph_plate():
  c ← canvas(320, 320), filled with paper;  font ← load_font(roboto.json)
  m ← text_matrix(font, 300, 40, 250)
  fill glyph_path(font, "a", m, 0.1) nonzero, in gray
  for contour in font.glyphs["a"].contours:
    hairline through every point of the contour, taken through m, closed          // width 1
  for contour in font.glyphs["a"].contours:
    for (x, y, on) in implied_points(contour):
      q ← m * point(x, y)
      if not on:  hollow circle at q, radius 4, in magenta       // circle_path(q, 4, 24) stroked 1.5 wide
      else if (x, y) is a point of the file's contour:  square at q, half-side 3, in cyan
      else:  square at q, half-side 2, in cyan                          // an implied point
  return c

plate_16():
  return magnify(glyph_plate(), 2)
