Chapter Five

Paths and Insideness

Draw a five-pointed star the way a child does, in one stroke, without lifting the pen. Now fill it in. Is the pentagon in the middle part of the star? Your pen went around it twice, so it's more inside than the points are, and also every drawing program you've ever used has a switch that makes it a hole. This chapter builds the path, the one data structure the rest of the book draws, and then asks it the question that turns out to have two answers.

Every shape so far has been a question: is this point inside the circle, the half-plane, the rectangle, the transformed union of segments. That was the right way to start, because it made antialiasing fall out of a better question rather than a smoothing pass, and it will keep being the reference renderer for the rest of the book. But nobody ships a tiger as a list of half-planes. They ship it as a path: move the pen here, draw a line to there, and there, and close. A path is a list of instructions, and the instructions don't say what's inside. That's something you have to decide, and it turns out that the decision is a convention, not a fact, and the two conventions in use disagree about the middle of a star.

§ 5.1 A path is a list of instructions

Three instructions, to start. move_to(p, point) lifts the pen and puts it down somewhere new. line_to(p, point) draws a straight line from wherever the pen is to the point. close(p) draws a line back to where the pen was last put down and marks the loop finished. Curves come in chapter 8 and are the same idea with more control points, so everything in this chapter survives them.

The structure that records this is a list of subpaths, one for each time the pen went down. A subpath is a list of points, in order, and a flag for whether it was closed. path() is empty. move_to appends a new subpath containing one point. line_to appends a point to the last subpath. close sets the flag. That's the whole data structure, and subpaths(p) hands it to the tests, each subpath with .points and .closed.

Then the edge cases, which are exactly the kind of thing that costs an afternoon if the book doesn't pin them, so it does. A line_to when the pen has never been put down behaves as a move_to: there's nothing to draw a line from, so it starts a subpath instead. A line_to after a close starts a new subpath, and it starts at the point the closed subpath began, because that's where close left the pen. PostScript and SVG both do this, and it's surprising the first time. A close with nothing to close does nothing, and closing twice is the same as closing once. Two move_tos in a row leave behind a subpath of one point, which is legal and draws nothing.

For filling, every subpath is treated as closed whether or not close was called. An open triangle has the same inside as a closed one. The flag matters to a stroke, in chapter 13, where an open path has two ends and a closed one doesn't. So edges(p), the list of every edge of every subpath as (a, b) pairs, always includes the edge from the last point back to the first, and a subpath of one point contributes no edges at all. A subpath of two points contributes two, there and back, and encloses nothing, which the winding number in the next section will confirm without any special case.

Last, bounds(p): the smallest axis-aligned box around every point of every subpath, as (min x, min y, max x, max y). For a polygon that's the bounding box of the shape; for a curve, in chapter 8, it'll be too big and need work. An empty path has no points, and the book says its bounds are (0, 0, 0, 0) rather than leaving you to discover what your language's min of nothing is. Two helpers the tests lean on: polygon(p1, p2, …) is a closed subpath through the points, and circle_path(cx, cy, r, n) is a regular n-gon standing in for a circle, its first point at angle 0, on the right, going clockwise on the screen. The circle is a polygon for now. Chapter 8 makes it honest.

Coordinates

Path coordinates are real numbers in the same space as chapter 2's shapes: pixel (x, y) is the unit square from x to x + 1 across and y to y + 1 down, and its center is at (x + 0.5, y + 0.5). A path vertex at (10, 10) sits on a pixel corner. Chapter 4's matrices apply to path points the way they applied to the F: transform the points, then ask about the result.

§ 5.2 Is this point inside?

Here's the oldest trick for it. Stand at the point and walk toward the right, forever, counting how many times you cross an edge of the polygon. Odd, and you started inside. Even, and you started outside. It works for any polygon, convex or not, in any number of pieces, because every time you cross an edge you go from in to out or out to in, and you end up outside.

To count, look at each edge a → b in turn. The ray is horizontal at height y, so the edge is in play only if it spans that height, and if it does, the point where it crosses the height is at x = a.x + t · (b.x - a.x) with t = (y - a.y) / (b.y - a.y). If that x is to the right of your point, you'll walk through it: count it. That's crossings(p, x, y), and it's ten lines, and it has a bug that every implementation of it has ever had.

The trap

The ray passes exactly through a vertex. Two edges meet there, and both span the ray's height if "spans" means min(a.y, b.y) ≤ y ≤ max(a.y, b.y), so both count, and a point that's inside comes out even. You might think this is rare. It isn't: a polygon with integer vertices tested at integer heights hits it constantly, and chapter 6 is going to test every polygon at every scanline, so any renderer that gets this wrong draws stray spikes on every flat-topped shape. The fix is to make the interval half-open. An edge spans the height y when a.y ≤ y < b.y or b.y ≤ y < a.y: the lower endpoint is in, the higher one is out. Now a vertex belongs to exactly one of its two edges when the polygon passes through it, going up on one side and down on the other. And when the vertex is a peak or a trough, both edges going the same way from it, both count or neither does, which is also right: a ray that grazes a peak crosses the boundary twice or not at all and stays on the side it started. One more thing falls out. A horizontal edge has a.y = b.y, and no y satisfies a.y ≤ y < a.y, so it's never crossed. You don't drop horizontal edges. The rule drops them for you.

The scenarios put a ray through a vertex of a diamond, from inside and from outside, and check that the count is 1 and 2. If you wrote ≤ at both ends you'll get 2 and 4. If you wrote < at both ends you'll get 0 and 0. Only the half-open rule gives the answer that means anything.

Crossings settle whether you're inside. They don't settle the star. Stand in the middle of the pentagram and walk right: you cross two edges, one of the arm you're heading into and one of the arm beyond it, even, outside. Stand in one of the points and you cross one, inside. So by this rule the star's center is a hole, and a pen that went around it twice counted for nothing. That's a defensible answer. It's also not the one you'd draw.

To get the other answer, give each crossing a sign. An edge that crosses your ray heading down the canvas, toward larger y, counts +1. One heading up counts -1. Sum them, and you have the winding number of the path around the point: how many times the path goes around you, net, and in which direction. A loop that goes around you once clockwise on the screen is +1. The same loop drawn the other way is -1. A loop drawn twice is 2. A loop that doesn't go around you at all is 0, no matter how many edges the ray crossed on the way out, because they cancel in pairs.

You can compute the sign from the crossing's x, as before, but there's a neater way that avoids the division, and chapter 4 built it: the cross product. For an edge a → b and your point q, the sign of cross(b - a, q - a) says which side of the edge q is on. An edge heading down with q to its left crosses your ray to the right of you; heading up, it's q to its right. So the whole thing is:

winding_at(p, x, y):
  q ← point(x, y)
  w ← 0
  for (a, b) in edges(p):
    if a.y ≤ y:                                   // heading down, q on the left
      if b.y > y and cross(b - a, q - a) > 0: w ← w + 1
    else:                                        // heading up, q on the right
      if b.y ≤ y and cross(b - a, q - a) < 0: w ← w - 1
  return w

Read the conditions again and you'll find the half-open rule hiding in them: the first branch takes edges with a.y ≤ y < b.y, the second takes b.y ≤ y < a.y, and a horizontal edge satisfies neither. Positive is clockwise on the screen, for the same reason chapter 4's cross product was: the canvas's y points down. A textbook with y up would call the same number counterclockwise. The arithmetic is identical; only the word changes, and the scenarios pin the number.

Where does it come out on the star? 1 in each of the five points, 2 in the middle, 0 outside. The crossing count is the same size in every region, it only forgot the signs, and for the star the signs happen to agree, so the difference between the two is exactly the difference between "2 is even" and "2 is not zero".

§ 5.3 Two rules

So there are two conventions, and every graphics system offers both. Nonzero: a point is inside when its winding number isn't zero. Even-odd: a point is inside when its winding number is odd. (Or its crossing count, which has the same parity; the winding number covers both, so you only need one function.) Odd means odd in absolute value: a loop drawn the other way round has winding -1, and that's inside under even-odd too. In languages where -3 % 2 is -1, and there are many, test w % 2 ≠ 0 rather than w % 2 = 1; a scenario winds a diamond three times backwards to make sure. They agree on any simple loop. They disagree whenever a path crosses itself or one subpath sits inside another, and the scenarios go through the cases one at a time.

A square with a smaller square inside it, drawn the opposite way round, is a ring under both rules: the inner loop winds -1 against the outer's +1, total 0, and zero is even. The same two squares drawn the same way round wind to 2 in the middle: a ring under even-odd, a solid square under nonzero. A loop drawn twice over itself is solid under nonzero and vanishes entirely under even-odd, which is a real thing that happens to real files when a path gets duplicated onto itself. And the pentagram: solid under nonzero, a hole in the middle under even-odd. The hole nobody drew.

Which is right? Neither. Nonzero is what you get by thinking about the pen. Even-odd is what you get by thinking about the ray. Nonzero has one large advantage that the book will collect in chapter 22: it survives boolean operations, because the union of two shapes is the path made of both their outlines, and under nonzero that's the union, while under even-odd the overlap becomes a hole. That's why nonzero is the default in SVG, PostScript and every font format. Even-odd survives because it's the one that lets you cut a hole without knowing which way round anything was drawn, which is a convenience people will not give up. Both rules are one line on top of winding_at, so write both.

To draw either, make it a shape. filled(p, rule), with rule either "nonzero" or "evenodd", is a shape whose inside is the rule applied to the path's winding number at the point. One more case in your inside from chapter 2, and every path you'll ever load can go through the same supersampler as the circle in chapter 2, the fan in chapter 3 and the F in chapter 4. That's the reference renderer this book promised, and it's complete now: any path, either rule, correct coverage, slowly.

One property of the half-open rule deserves a scenario of its own, because it's the reason rasterizers use it. Take the square from (0, 0) to (10, 10) and ask about points exactly on its boundary. The top edge and the left edge are inside: winding 1. The bottom edge and the right edge are outside: winding 0. That's not an accident of the arithmetic, it's the rule doing its job. Now put two rectangles side by side, sharing the edge x = 5. A point on the shared edge is inside exactly one of them, so the winding there is 1, not 2 and not 0. Tiles that share an edge neither overlap nor leave a gap. Chapter 9 will have things to say about how this interacts with antialiasing, but at the level of "is this sample inside", the half-open rule is what makes a mosaic possible.

Finally, a small kindness to your CPU. rasterize(shape, w, h) asks every pixel of the buffer 64 questions, whether the shape is anywhere near it or not. Now that paths have bounds, rasterize_within(shape, box, w, h) visits only the pixels the box touches: columns from floor(min x) up to but not including ceil(max x), rows likewise, clipped to the buffer, and leaves the rest at zero. Same coverage, less work, and a scenario checks the two agree on the star. It's a stopgap. Chapters 6 and 7 replace this whole approach, and the reason to have it now is that the plate renders in seconds instead of a minute.

In the GUI

Illustrator's Attributes panel has two buttons, Use Non-Zero Winding Fill Rule and Use Even-Odd Fill Rule, that switch filled's second argument for the selected path. Beside them, Reverse Path Direction flips a subpath's winding, which under nonzero makes a hole appear or vanish. Inkscape puts the same pair under Fill & Stroke → Fill.

§ 5.4 Putting it together

The pentagram, filled four ways. star() is five points on a circle of radius 70 about (80.5, 80.5), the first straight up, visited every second one so the pen crosses itself, and closed. Its vertices are pinned to four decimals so a star drawn the other way round, or starting at the wrong point, fails before it renders. Then a panel per rule per method: the center question from chapter 2 for the jagged pair, chapter 2's coverage within the star's bounds for the smooth pair, and the four side by side and stacked, magnified twice:

star():
  p ← path()
  for k in 0..4:
    a ← (-90 + 144 * k) degrees
    q ← point(80.5 + 70 * cos(a), 80.5 + 70 * sin(a))
    if k = 0: move_to(p, q) else: line_to(p, q)
  close(p)
  return p

star_panel(rule, method):
  c ← canvas(160, 160)
  fill(c, color(0.02, 0.02, 0.025))
  s ← filled(star(), rule)
  if method = "centers": cov ← rasterize_centers(s, 160, 160)
  else:                  cov ← rasterize_within(s, bounds(star()), 160, 160)
  paint_through(c, cov, color(0.9, 0.55, 0.1))
  return c

star_centers():  return side_by_side(star_panel("nonzero", "centers"),  star_panel("evenodd", "centers"))
star_coverage(): return side_by_side(star_panel("nonzero", "coverage"), star_panel("evenodd", "coverage"))

plate_05():
  top ← star_centers()
  bottom ← star_coverage()
  both ← canvas(320, 320)
  for y in 0..159:
    for x in 0..319:
      write_pixel(both, x, y,       pixel_at(top, x, y))
      write_pixel(both, x, y + 160, pixel_at(bottom, x, y))
  return magnify(both, 2)

The coverage panels are two rasterizations of a 160-by-160 canvas at 64 samples a pixel, each sample a winding number over five edges, within the star's bounds: a few seconds in Python, well under a second compiled. The center panels are 64 times cheaper and look it. Every panel scenario probes the middle of the star, where the two rules part company, and a point in an arm, where they agree, and a pixel on the star's top edge where only the coverage rows have a value between 0 and 1.
