Chapter Three

Lines

You already own a line renderer. A line is a very thin rectangle, a rectangle is four half-planes, and chapter 2 will rasterize any shape you can describe. It does twenty million inside tests to draw twelve of them. This chapter is about why every renderer in history refused to pay that, what they did instead, and why you're going to stop doing it anyway.

Lines are the oldest problem in computer graphics, and the first good answer is from 1962. Jack Bresenham was working on a plotter, and the question was how to move a pen that could only step one unit at a time along a path that wasn't aligned to the steps. His answer used no multiplication, no division and no floating point, because the machine had none of those to spare, and it has been copied into every graphics library written since. It's beautiful, and you should write it once.

Then you should write Xiaolin Wu's version from 1991, which looks enormously better for about four more lines of code. And then you should notice what Wu's version is actually computing, because once you see it you'll understand why the rest of this book never special-cases a line again.

§ 3.1 Bresenham

Here's the idea. A line from (x0, y0) to (x1, y1), with integer endpoints, and suppose for now it's more horizontal than vertical. Then it visits every column from x0 to x1 exactly once, and in each column there's exactly one pixel to light: the one whose center is nearest the ideal line. Walk along x one step at a time, and keep track of how far the ideal line has drifted from the row you're on. When the drift passes half a pixel, step to the next row and subtract a pixel from the drift.

The trick that makes it integer-only is to scale the drift by dx, so that "half a pixel" is dx / 2 and "the line rose by dy / dx" is "add dy". Every quantity is a whole number and the comparison is against zero. That's the whole algorithm, and here it is, complete, because the book asks you to reproduce its exact pixels and there's more than one way to break ties:

line_bresenham(c, x0, y0, x1, y1, col):          // ranges are inclusive, as always
  steep ← |y1 - y0| > |x1 - x0|
  if steep:   swap x0 with y0, and x1 with y1        // walk along y instead
  if x0 > x1: swap (x0, y0) with (x1, y1)             // always walk left to right

  dx    ← x1 - x0
  dy    ← |y1 - y0|
  ystep ← y0 < y1 ? 1 : -1
  err   ← dx / 2                                       // integer division
  y     ← y0

  for x in x0..x1:
    if steep: write_pixel(c, y, x, col)               // swap back on the way out
    else:     write_pixel(c, x, y, col)
    err ← err - dy
    if err < 0:
      y   ← y + ystep
      err ← err + dx

Two things in there are load-bearing. The steep swap makes a line that's more vertical than horizontal walk along y instead of x, and then swaps the coordinates back when it writes each pixel. Without it a steep line has gaps, because each column would get one pixel when it needs several. The left-to-right swap means the pixels you get don't depend on which end you called the start, which is a property you'll want in chapter 6 when polygon edges arrive in whatever order the file put them in.

The tie rule falls out of err ← dx / 2 rounded down when dx is odd, together with the strict err < 0: when the ideal line passes exactly halfway between two rows, this version stays on the row it's on for one more step. Round the half up, or test err ≤ 0, and it steps early instead. Other books do; neither is more right, and the tests pin this one. Whether you keep err as an integer or a real number doesn't change a single pixel, as long as you round the half down.

To test a line you need to see which pixels it lit. lit_pixels(c) returns every pixel of the canvas that isn't black, with the usual tolerance, as a list of (x, y) pairs in reading order: top row first, left to right within a row. The tolerance matters: a slope of a third lands one of Wu's weights at 0.0000000000000002, and a pixel that faint isn't lit. It's a test helper, not a renderer function, and it's a few lines.

Now draw something with it. Twelve rays from the center of a 160-by-160 canvas to points 72 pixels out, one every 30 degrees, in an off-white on a near-black paper. The endpoints are rounded to integers, and ray_ends() returns them so the tests can check yours match. None of the twelve lands on a half, so the tie rule of your round doesn't matter here, and the scenario would tell you if it did:

ray_ends():
  ends ← []
  for k in 0..11:
    a ← k * 30 degrees
    ends.append( (round(80 + 72 * cos(a)), round(80 + 72 * sin(a))) )
  return ends

fan_bresenham():
  c ← canvas(160, 160)
  fill(c, color(0.02, 0.02, 0.025))
  for (x, y) in ray_ends():
    line_bresenham(c, 80, 80, x, y, color(0.92, 0.92, 0.88))
  return c

Open it and look at the rays. The horizontal and vertical ones are solid bars. Every slanted one is a repeating pattern of short runs: at 30° the line rises 0.577 pixels per column, so it stays on a row for two columns, then one, then two, and the pattern recurs all the way out. Your eye picks up the period and reads it as a texture, and at 60° the same thing happens down the other axis. Every ray is drawn with the same rule and the same color, and they don't look like the same line.

§ 3.2 Wu

The fix is almost embarrassingly small. In each column, the ideal line sits at some y that isn't a whole number, say y = 4.3. Bresenham lights row 4 and ignores the 0.3. Wu lights row 4 at 70% and row 5 at 30%. Two pixels per column instead of one, with weights that always sum to 1, and the beads join up into a line.

To light a pixel at a weight, you paint it: mix(pixel_at(c, x, y), col, weight, true), written back with write_pixel. That's paint_through for one pixel, in light whatever the global switch says, for the same reason, and it's worth its own little function, plot(c, x, y, col, weight), that also drops writes off the canvas and, as a shortcut rather than a rule, skips a weight of zero. The bounds check in plot is load-bearing even though write_pixel has one of its own, because plot has to read the pixel first and pixel_at makes no promises outside the canvas. With integer endpoints you can compute the ideal y directly in floating point, as y0 + (x - x0) * slope, and split it with floor. Floor, not truncation toward zero: they agree on positive numbers and part company below zero, and a line that starts above the canvas has a negative y for a few columns. Casting to an integer is the port bug this chapter sees most, and a scenario starts a line at row -1 to catch it. The same steep swap and left-to-right swap as before apply, for the same reasons, and a line of one point has dx = 0, which the slope guards against.

line_wu(c, x0, y0, x1, y1, col):
  steep ← |y1 - y0| > |x1 - x0|
  if steep:   swap x0 with y0, and x1 with y1
  if x0 > x1: swap (x0, y0) with (x1, y1)

  dx    ← x1 - x0
  slope ← dx = 0 ? 0 : (y1 - y0) / dx                 // real division

  for x in x0..x1:
    y  ← y0 + (x - x0) * slope
    yi ← floor(y)
    f  ← y - yi
    if steep: plot(c, yi, x, col, 1 - f);  plot(c, yi + 1, x, col, f)
    else:     plot(c, x, yi, col, 1 - f);  plot(c, x, yi + 1, col, f)

Wu's original handles endpoints that aren't on pixel centers, and that's where every implementation quietly breaks: the first and last steps need partial weights of their own, and near-horizontal and near-vertical lines expose any mistake in them. This chapter sidesteps all of it by allowing integer endpoints only. Chapter 4 brings real coordinates, and by then you won't be using this function.

To measure a line rather than look at it, sum the paint. total_ink(c) adds up the red channel of every pixel on the canvas, which for a white line on black is exactly how much paint went down. For Wu it's always the number of columns, because each column's two weights sum to 1. Keep that in mind for the next section.

Notice where the shortfall comes from. Every column still carries exactly one unit of paint, so no pixel is dimmer than it should be; a slanted line of the same length covers fewer columns, so there are fewer units. Wu's rays look smooth, but the slanted ones are carrying up to 18% less paint than the horizontal ones, and if you squint at the fan you can see it: the rays at 30° and 60° are thinner than the axes.

§ 3.3 The reveal

Look at what Wu's weights are. In a column, a one-pixel-wide band centered on the ideal line covers 70% of one pixel and 30% of the next, more or less. Wu's algorithm is computing the coverage of a thin rectangle, badly, one column at a time. The 18% is the price of the "more or less": a rectangle one pixel wide and ten long has area 10 at any angle, and Wu's two-pixels-per-column rule can't see that. In a column, the band's slice is a parallelogram with area 1 / cos θ, more than 1 as soon as the line tilts, and Wu hands every column exactly 1. The undercount is cos θ, which for the 3-4-5 lines in the outline is 0.8 on one axis and the missing tenth of the paint on the other.

So describe the rectangle honestly and let the supersampler do the work. thick_line(x0, y0, x1, y1, width) is a shape: the rectangle of that width centered on the segment from the center of pixel (x0, y0) to the center of pixel (x1, y1), with square ends. A line of no length has no direction, so it gets (1, 0) and its two ends are pushed apart by half the width each, which makes it a width-by-width square; there's a scenario, because chapter 6 will hand you degenerate edges. Build it from four half-planes. With d the unit direction of the segment and n the unit normal, (-d.y, d.x), the four are: through the start point facing along d; through the end point facing back along -d; and one along each side, offset by half the width along n, facing inward. A point is inside the rectangle when it's inside all four. Your inside from chapter 2 grows one case, and width is a real number that this chapter mostly sets to 1; one scenario uses 3 so the parameter isn't decorative. Since the signs are the kind of thing that costs an evening, here it is in full:

thick_line(x0, y0, x1, y1, width):
  ax, ay ← x0 + 0.5, y0 + 0.5                // pixel centers
  bx, by ← x1 + 0.5, y1 + 0.5
  h ← width / 2
  if length = 0:                             // no direction: make it a square
    dx, dy ← 1, 0
    ax, bx ← ax - h, bx + h
  else:
    dx, dy ← (bx - ax, by - ay) / length     // unit direction
  nx, ny ← -dy, dx                           // unit normal
  return intersection of
    half_plane(ax, ay, dx, dy),               // ahead of the start
    half_plane(bx, by, -dx, -dy),             // behind the end
    half_plane(ax + nx*h, ay + ny*h, -nx, -ny),   // inside the left side
    half_plane(ax - nx*h, ay - ny*h, nx, ny)      // inside the right side

Then the scenarios. A horizontal thick line covers its row fully, with half a pixel at each end, because the segment runs from pixel center to pixel center: ink 7 for a line 7 long. The half-pixel matters, and a line at a slant is the only kind that can tell: build the rectangle from pixel corners instead and every horizontal, vertical and 45° test still passes, so one scenario pins a line from (2, 2) to (11, 5) pixel by pixel. And the outline: four lines of length 10, at four angles, and every one of them has ink 10. Not 11, not 9. The line has become an area, and an area doesn't care which way it's pointing.

The diagonal scenario keeps you honest. The 45° line of length 9.9 comes out at 9.71875, off by nearly 2%, and it's the same grid blindness from chapter 2: the rectangle's edges run along the diagonals of the sample grid. Everything in Part II fixes this properly. For now it's a known number, and it's a great deal better than 18%.

The trap

That 9.71875 is 622 samples out of 640, and four of those samples sit exactly on the half-plane through the line's start: their coordinates are sixteenths, and the two products in the dot product cancel to zero in exact arithmetic. Whether your machine agrees depends on how it multiplies. Many compilers fuse a * b + c * d into a single fused multiply-add, which keeps one product at full precision and turns the exact zero into -0.00000000000000005, and four samples that should count as inside fall out. You'll see 9.65625 and no idea why. Whether it happens is a compiler setting, not a property of your machine: clang contracts by default, several others don't, and the same source gives different answers under different flags. The fix is to switch contraction off for the renderer (#pragma STDC FP_CONTRACT OFF, or the equivalent flag), and to remember, every time a scenario puts a sample on a boundary, that "boundary included" is a promise your arithmetic has to keep exactly.

It's slow. Twelve rasterizations of a 160-by-160 canvas at 64 samples a pixel is twenty million inside tests: ten to twenty seconds in Python or Ruby, a quarter of a second or less in most compiled languages, half a minute in one whose compiler doesn't inline closures, and every way about a thousand times the work Bresenham did for the same picture. Every ray is the same weight now. Nothing flickers, nothing beads, and the slanted rays are as heavy as the axes. The fan scenarios probe a pixel on the steep ray at 60° as well as the shallow one at 30°, because a Bresenham that forgot its steep swap draws the shallow rays perfectly. Then go back and look at the work, because that's the actual lesson of this chapter.

Bresenham's and Wu's algorithms are fast because they know they're drawing a line and exploit everything about it. The rectangle is slow because chapter 2's rasterizer knows nothing about anything: it asks every pixel on the canvas 64 questions, whether the shape is anywhere near it or not. But the rectangle is right, and it's right for the same reason it's right for circles and, from chapter 5, for every polygon and curve you'll ever draw. Special-purpose primitives are a local optimum. The book spent this chapter there so it could leave on purpose, and the way out isn't a faster line algorithm. It's a faster rasterizer, which is what chapters 6 and 7 are.

In the GUI

The Pencil and the Brush, in Photoshop and every raster editor since MacPaint. A 1px Pencil is §3.1 with the antialiasing switched off, which is exactly why pixel artists reach for it: they want the staircase. The Brush at 1px is §3.3, a coverage-filled stroke, and it never gives you a crisp pixel no matter how hard you try.

§ 3.4 Putting it together

Bresenham's fan and Wu's, side by side, magnified twice so the beads and the weights are visible at arm's length:

plate_03():
  both ← canvas(320, 160)
  a ← fan_bresenham()
  b ← fan_wu()
  for y in 0..159:
    for x in 0..159:
      write_pixel(both, x,       y, pixel_at(a, x, y))
      write_pixel(both, x + 160, y, pixel_at(b, x, y))
  return magnify(both, 2)
