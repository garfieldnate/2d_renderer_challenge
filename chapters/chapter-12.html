Chapter Twelve

Clipping, Masks and Groups

Two things are left before the machine is whole: deciding where paint is allowed to land, and combining whole stacks of it as one. The first is clipping, and it comes with a small gift — it needs almost no new code, because the thing a clip is made of is the coverage buffer you built in chapter 2. A clip is a shape's coverage; clipping is multiplying two coverage buffers together; and nesting clips is multiplying more of them, which composes for free. The second is groups, and it hides a genuine subtlety that every designer has hit without knowing its name: turning a group's opacity down is not the same as turning each thing inside it down, and the difference is exactly the overlaps.

This is the chapter where chapter 2's one design decision pays its last dividend. Making coverage a first-class value — a buffer you can hold, not a thing hidden inside the fill — meant that a clip, a mask, and the alpha of a rendered layer are all the same kind of object, and combining any of them is arithmetic on buffers. Nothing here is a new algorithm. It's four small operations on things you already have, and a bounding box you have to remember to compute.

§ 12.1 A clip is a coverage buffer

To clip a fill to a region, you want its paint only where the region is. You already produce, for every fill, a coverage buffer that's the fraction of each pixel the shape covers. A clip is the same thing — a coverage buffer for the clip region — and to apply it you multiply the two, cell by cell. multiply_coverage(a, b) is the whole operation. Where the clip is 1 the fill passes untouched; where the clip is 0 it's erased; and the antialiased edge of the clip softens the fill's edge for free, because it's a number between 0 and 1 like any other. clip_rect and clip_path are only fills that produce a clip, and there was never a difference between a clip and a shape.

Two properties fall straight out of multiplication, and they're the tests. full_clip is coverage 1 everywhere, so multiplying by it changes nothing: clipping to the whole canvas is a no-op, which sounds trivial until a bug makes it not one. And multiplication commutes, so nesting a circle clip inside a rectangle clip gives the identical result to nesting them the other way. A clip stack is a running product, and the order you pushed the clips never matters.

§ 12.2 Soft masks

Once a clip is a coverage buffer, a soft clip is free: fill it with values between 0 and 1 instead of only 0 and 1, and the same multiplication fades the shape instead of cutting it. That's a mask. soft_mask here is a radial falloff — 1 at its centre, dropping to 0 at a radius — and multiplying a shape by it dissolves the shape's edge into the background, the vignette every photo app ships. The mask can come from anywhere a coverage buffer can: a gradient's alpha, an image's brightness, another shape's antialiased fill. The renderer doesn't care where the numbers came from; it multiplies them in.

Both halves are the same star — five points on a radius-60 circle about (75, 75) — multiplied by a clip: on the left the coverage of circle_path(75, 75, 45, 64), on the right soft_mask(75, 75, 70, …), the radial falloff. The result is painted through with one ink. That's clip_demo, and the only difference between the two panels is which coverage buffer the star was multiplied by.

features/chapter12-mask.feature

Feature: Soft masks
  A soft mask is a clip whose values are between 0 and 1 instead of only 0 or
  1, and it multiplies in exactly the same way. soft_mask is a radial falloff,
  coverage 1 at its center fading to 0 at radius r, so multiplying a shape by
  it fades the shape out toward the edge instead of cutting it hard.

    When  masked ← multiply_coverage(shape, m)
    Then  coverage_at(masked, 5, 5) = 0.8586 ± 0.0001
    And   coverage_at(masked, 0, 0) = 0

§ 12.3 Groups, and the opacity that isn't what you think

The last operation is combining a stack of drawing as one thing. You draw the children into an offscreen layer — push_group makes a fresh transparent one, chapter 9's premultiplied buffer — then composite that whole layer back onto the canvas. On its own that's not interesting. It becomes interesting when the group carries an opacity. Lowering a group's opacity should fade the group as a single flattened object. Lowering each child's opacity fades them one at a time. Those are different pictures, and the difference lives entirely in the overlaps.

Here's why, and it's worth doing slowly. Say two half-transparent shapes overlap. Draw them at per-child opacity and the overlap gets composited twice — the second shape lays its half over the already-half-covered first — so the overlap is darker and more saturated than either shape alone. Now put them in a group at full opacity, so inside the group they're opaque and the top one wins the overlap, then drop the group to half: every pixel of the flattened group, overlap and all, fades by the same half. The overlap now matches its surroundings. The group flattened before the opacity applied, so the double-composite never happened.

pop_group_with_opacity(group, base, opacity):
  faded ← scale_opacity(group, opacity)     // every premultiplied channel × opacity
  return composite(src-over, faded, base)   // the whole group, once

// at opacity 1 this is pixel-identical to drawing the children straight onto base:
// scaling by 1 does nothing, and compositing the group is compositing its children.

The trap

A group buffer is a whole extra layer, and the lazy thing is to make it canvas-sized. Do that and a page with fifty nested groups allocates fifty full-canvas buffers — on a large document, gigabytes, for groups that each cover a postage stamp. The fix is unglamorous and mandatory: allocate the group buffer to the bounding box of what it will hold, offset everything into that box, and composite it back at its offset. Getting the bounds right, and propagating them up through nested groups and clips so a parent knows how big its child came out, is half the real work of this chapter and none of the fun. The book keeps its groups canvas-sized because its canvases are small and the code stays readable; a production renderer cannot, and the bounding-box arithmetic is where clipping stops being free.

Feature: Groups
  A group is an offscreen layer you draw children into, then composite back at
  one opacity. push_group starts it; paint_into draws a child; scale_opacity
  lowers the whole layer's alpha; pop_group_with_opacity flattens the group and
  composites it at an opacity. At opacity 1 a group is identical to drawing its
  children straight onto the canvas — but below 1 it differs, because the group

  resolved rather than composited twice.

    Given a ← polygon(point(2, 2), point(12, 2), point(12, 12), point(2, 12))
    And   b ← polygon(point(6, 6), point(16, 6), point(16, 16), point(6, 16))

    And   cb ← fill_path(b, "nonzero", 20, 20)
    When  perchild ← paint_into(paint_into(layer(20, 20), ca, color(1, 0, 0), 0.5), cb, color(0, 0, 1), 0.5)
    And   group ← paint_into(paint_into(push_group(20, 20), ca, color(1, 0, 0), 1.0), cb, color(0, 0, 1), 1.0)
    And   grouped ← pop_group_with_opacity(group, layer(20, 20), 0.5)
    Then  layer_pixel(grouped, 3, 3) = layer_pixel(perchild, 3, 3)
    And   layer_pixel(grouped, 8, 8) ≠ layer_pixel(perchild, 8, 8)

§ 12.4 Putting it together

The plate is the group-opacity surprise, side by side. Three overlapping circles in three inks. On the left, each is painted onto the canvas at half opacity, and the overlaps go dark and muddy where the paint stacks. On the right, the three are drawn opaque into a group and the whole group is composited at half opacity, and the overlaps are the same clean strength as everywhere else. Same circles, same 50%, one is applied per shape and the other to the flattened group.

per_child():                                  // left: 50% per circle
  base ← opaque paper
  for circle, colour in the three:
    base ← paint_into(base, fill_path(circle), colour, 0.5)
  return base

group_opacity():                              // right: opaque group, 50% once
  base  ← opaque paper
  group ← push_group()
  for circle, colour in the three:
    group ← paint_into(group, fill_path(circle), colour, 1.0)
  return pop_group_with_opacity(group, base, 0.5)

Clipping was multiplication, masks were the same multiplication with softer numbers, and a group was a layer composited once. All of it rode on the coverage buffer from chapter 2 and the compositing from chapter 9. That closes Part III: coverage says where, paint says what colour, compositing says how they combine, and clips and groups say where it's allowed and how it stacks. The renderer can now draw any filled, gradient-painted, image-textured, clipped, grouped shape you can describe. What it still can't do is draw a line with width — a stroke — and that turns out to be a fill of a shape you have to construct, which is Part IV.
