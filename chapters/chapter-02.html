Chapter Two

Coverage

Draw a circle. You have a canvas and a way to write pixels, so the plan is obvious: for every pixel, decide whether it's in the circle or not, and color the ones that are. That plan is wrong, and the way it's wrong is the subject of the next twenty-two chapters.

Here's the trouble. A pixel isn't a point. It's a little square, and a circle's edge doesn't care about little squares: it slices through them wherever it likes, leaving a third of one inside and two thirds of the next. Ask "is this pixel inside?" and you've forced a yes-or-no answer onto a question whose honest answer is "40%". Stack a few hundred of those forced answers along an edge and you get the staircase everybody recognizes.

The right question is "how much of this pixel is inside?" and its answer is a number between 0 and 1 called coverage. This chapter builds a buffer to hold coverage, a brutally simple way to compute it, and a way to push paint through it onto the canvas. The picture at the end is the same circle drawn both ways, side by side, and once you've seen it you won't want to go back.

Everything after this chapter is a faster way to compute the same number.

§ 2.1 Shapes are questions

Before you can ask how much of a pixel is inside a shape, you need shapes, and you need them to answer one question: is this point inside you? That's the whole interface. A shape is a function from a point to yes or no, and for this chapter, three of them will do.

  • A circle, given its center and radius. Inside means within the radius, boundary included.
  • A rectangle, given its left, top, right and bottom edges as rectangle(x0, y0, x1, y1). Boundary included.
  • A half-plane, which is everything on one side of a line. You describe it with a point on the line and a normal vector pointing into the half you want: half_plane(px, py, nx, ny). A point is inside when the vector from (px, py) to it has a non-negative dot product with the normal. The normal needn't have length 1. Half-planes look useless now and turn out to be the building block of chapter 3.

Coordinates are real numbers here, not pixel indices. The point (3, 3) is a mathematical point; the pixel whose top-left corner is at (3, 3) is a different thing, and keeping those two apart is most of what this chapter is about.

§ 2.2 A faster file

A quick detour, because the pictures in this chapter are about to get bigger. PPM has a binary sibling, P6. Same three header lines, with a 6 in place of the 3, then exactly one newline, then the pixel data as raw bytes: one byte per channel, nothing in between. A 2-by-1 canvas is 11 bytes of header and 6 bytes of pixels. The file is somewhere between a quarter and a third the size of the P3 version, depending on how many three-digit values it had, and it's considerably faster to write. From here on the book's reference images are P6.

Write canvas_to_p6. The bytes are the same numbers your P3 writer computes, clamp then encode then scale then round, so share that code. Then teach ppm_pixel, distinct_values and max_channel_difference to read either format: look at the first two bytes, and if they're P6, read the three header numbers, skip the single whitespace byte after the last one, and take the rest as pixel bytes. Your read_file now returns bytes rather than text; a P3 file is text that happens to be stored in bytes, so nothing changes for chapter 1's tests. In a language that keeps strings and byte arrays firmly apart, make the three readers take bytes and convert a P3 string at the call, or overload them; the scenarios compare a P3 to a P6 directly, so both routes have to end in the same place. The size rule from chapter 1 still holds: files of different dimensions differ by 255, whatever their format.

One trap, and it's the reason the reader can't reuse chapter 1's "split on whitespace": a pixel byte can be 10 or 32, which are the newline and space characters. Split a P6 file the way you split a P3 and those pixels vanish. Read the header byte by byte until you've seen three numbers and the one whitespace after the last, and treat every byte after that as pixel data no matter what it looks like. There's a scenario whose first pixel is 10 32 10 for exactly this reason. Bytes in the scenarios are numbered from 1, the same way lines are: byte 1 is the P, and in a 2-by-1 file byte 12 is the first pixel byte. length is a count of bytes, not characters. And the binary writer shares the P3 writer's clamp; there's a scenario that feeds it a 1.5.

Keep the P3 writer. When something goes wrong, you'll still want to cat a file.

§ 2.3 A loupe

The discs in this chapter are 40 pixels across, because 40-pixel discs are quick to render and small enough to reason about pixel by pixel. They're also too small to see. So the second tool is a magnifier: magnify(canvas, k) returns a canvas k times wider and taller, with every pixel repeated into a k-by-k block. No smoothing, no averaging, no cleverness. Image viewers do this when you zoom in, but doing it yourself means the file on disk is what you see, and you're not at the mercy of a viewer that decides to "improve" the zoom with a blur. The scenario magnifies a 2-by-1 canvas rather than a square one on purpose: a magnifier with x and y swapped produces a perfect picture of any shape that's symmetric about the diagonal, which the discs later in this chapter are, so this is the one place the swap gets caught.

§ 2.4 The coverage buffer, and the first question

A coverage buffer is a canvas of numbers instead of colors. It has a width and a height, and one number per pixel, from 0 for "none of this pixel is inside" to 1 for "all of it". It starts at zero. You read it with coverage_at and write it with set_coverage, and as with the canvas, writes outside the buffer are dropped and reads outside it are your problem. Nothing in the book reads out of bounds, and paint_through is only ever called with a canvas and a buffer of the same size. Its total, ink, is the sum of every value, and you'll use it constantly: it's the area of the shape, in pixels, as the buffer sees it.

The first way to fill one is the binary question. Pixel (x, y) is the square from (x, y) to (x + 1, y + 1), so its center is (x + 0.5, y + 0.5). Ask the shape whether the center is inside. If so, coverage 1; if not, 0. That's center_inside(shape, x, y), and it returns the number, not a boolean, because the number is what goes in the buffer. Do that for every pixel and you've got rasterize_centers, which is what every renderer did until the nineties and what a few still do when you turn antialiasing off.

That half-pixel offset is the one thing here that people get wrong. If you test (x, y) instead of (x + 0.5, y + 0.5), every shape you ever draw will sit half a pixel up and to the left of where it should, and you'll never notice until you draw the same shape two ways and they don't line up. There's a scenario for it.

§ 2.5 Paint through it

A coverage buffer is a stencil. To get a picture out of it you push paint through: paint_through(canvas, coverage, color) moves every pixel of the canvas toward the color by that pixel's coverage. Zero coverage leaves a pixel alone, full coverage replaces it, and in between it's mix(pixel, color, coverage) with the switch forced to the light's way: mix(pixel, color, coverage, true). The browser-style switch exists so you can compare gradients against other tools; it has no business inside the rasterizer, and a scenario turns it off and checks that painting doesn't notice. This is the one place the renderer touches the canvas, from here to the end of the book.

That last sentence is worth reading again. Chapters 3 through 8 are about producing better coverage buffers faster. Chapters 9 through 12 are about producing fancier paint. Neither side needs to know about the other, and the seam between them is this one function.

  fill(c, color(0.02, 0.02, 0.025))

Write it out as P6, open it, and look at the edge. Every step is a full pixel. It's a perfectly good circle and it looks like it was drawn in 1985, because this is exactly how circles were drawn in 1985.

§ 2.6 The better question

Now ask how much of the pixel is inside. The honest answer is an area, and computing the exact area of a pixel clipped by a circle is a genuinely annoying piece of geometry that you're not going to do. Instead, you're going to cheat in the most obvious way possible: throw a handful of points into the pixel and count how many land inside the shape.

Specifically: divide the pixel into an 8-by-8 grid and put one sample point at the center of each cell, so the sample in row j, column i of pixel (x, y) is at (x + (i + 0.5) / 8, y + (j + 0.5) / 8). Ask the shape about all 64. Coverage is the count divided by 64. That's coverage(shape, x, y), and rasterize(shape, w, h) is a buffer full of it.

It's slow. Sixty-four shape queries per pixel, for every pixel on the canvas, whether the shape is anywhere near it or not. You won't feel it on a 40-pixel disc; on a 1920-by-1080 canvas it's 133 million questions per shape. It's also correct to within 1/64 for an axis-aligned edge, and off by at most 1/16 on the worst diagonal, for reasons the next paragraph gets to, and it's so simple that it's very hard to get wrong. That combination is why it stays in the book for good: from here on, whenever a chapter builds a faster rasterizer, this one is what the fast one gets checked against. It's your reference renderer, and you've written it in a dozen lines.

The rectangle scenario below is exact, not approximate: its edges at 1.25 and 4.75 land on sample boundaries, so 6 of the 8 columns of samples fall inside and the coverage is 0.75 on the nose. The half-plane scenarios show the method's one honest weakness. A vertical line through a pixel center covers exactly half the samples, and so does a line at most angles. But a line at exactly 45° through the center runs straight through a diagonal of sample points, every one of which counts as inside, and the answer comes out 0.5625. A regular grid has a few directions it's blind to; renderers that care use a rotated or jittered grid. You'll live with it, and you'll know what it is when you see it.

The disc's area, remember, is 78.54. The center test said 80. The sample test says 78.5, which is why the scenario asserts the ink twice: once as the exact number the buffer holds, and once against the true area with a tenth of a pixel to spare. The difference isn't the point. The point is the edge. Notice what didn't happen. Nobody blurred anything. There's no smoothing pass, no filter, no post-processing. The edge pixels are the colors they are because that's how much of each pixel the circle covers. Antialiasing isn't something you do to a picture afterwards. It's what you get when you ask the right question in the first place.

In the GUI

The Anti-alias checkbox in the options bar of Photoshop's Elliptical Marquee, Lasso and shape tools. Off is §2.4; on is §2.6. It's off by default for the rectangular marquee, because a rectangle on integer coordinates covers every pixel fully or not at all, and it's on for everything else.

§ 2.7 Coverage is not opacity

Here is the trap, and unlike chapter 1's it doesn't announce itself.

Paint the disc once through its coverage. An edge pixel with coverage 0.5 ends up halfway between the paper and the orange. Now paint the same disc again, through the same coverage. The interior doesn't change: it was fully orange and it stays fully orange. But that edge pixel moves halfway again, from 0.5 to 0.75, and the edge of the disc gets visibly fatter. Paint it ten times and the disc grows a hard rim.

Nothing is wrong with the code. The problem is a confusion of two different quantities that look identical. Coverage says how much of the pixel the shape occupies. Opacity says how much of the light the paint lets through. A pixel half covered by opaque paint and a pixel fully covered by half-transparent paint end up the same color, and every renderer stores them the same way, as an alpha of 0.5. But they're not the same thing, and the difference bites the moment two shapes share an edge. Two adjacent shapes with coverage 0.5 on the same pixel should make that pixel 100% painted; composite them as two 50% opacities and you get 75%, and a faint seam runs along every boundary in your drawing. Every 2D renderer on earth has that seam and nobody mentions it. Chapter 9 does.

For now, render it, so you'll recognize it later.

painted_twice():
  c ← canvas(80, 40)
  fill(c, color(0.02, 0.02, 0.025))
  cov ← rasterize(circle(20, 20, 16), 40, 40)

  once ← coverage_buffer(80, 40)         // the disc in both halves
  for y in 0..39:
    for x in 0..39:
      set_coverage(once, x, y,      coverage_at(cov, x, y))
      set_coverage(once, x + 40, y, coverage_at(cov, x, y))
  paint_through(c, once, color(0.9, 0.55, 0.1))

  twice ← coverage_buffer(80, 40)        // the disc in the right half only
  for y in 0..39:
    for x in 0..39:
      set_coverage(twice, x + 40, y, coverage_at(cov, x, y))
  paint_through(c, twice, color(0.9, 0.55, 0.1))

  return magnify(c, 6)

§ 2.8 Putting it together

The same circle, the same grid, the two questions. Centers on the left, coverage on the right.

plate_02():
  c ← canvas(80, 40)
  fill(c, color(0.02, 0.02, 0.025))
  shape ← circle(20, 20, 16)
  left  ← rasterize_centers(shape, 40, 40)
  right ← rasterize(shape, 40, 40)

  both ← coverage_buffer(80, 40)
  for y in 0..39:
    for x in 0..39:
      set_coverage(both, x, y,      coverage_at(left, x, y))
      set_coverage(both, x + 40, y, coverage_at(right, x, y))

  paint_through(c, both, color(0.9, 0.55, 0.1))
  return magnify(c, 6)
