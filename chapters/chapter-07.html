Chapter Seven

Analytic Antialiasing

Chapter 6's fill asks one question per pixel: is your center inside? For a fat shape that's the right question and the answer is a clean staircase. For a thin one it's a disaster. A needle a third of a pixel wide passes between the centers and lights nothing; shift it over and it lights a single hard dot. The picture on the left is twelve of them, and it's more gap than needle. This chapter answers a better question — how much of each pixel is inside — and it answers it exactly, in one pass, with two numbers per cell and no sampling anywhere. It's the rasterizer inside every font engine you've ever read text through, and it's about a page of code.

The staircase in chapter 6 wasn't a bug. A pixel is either inside or it isn't, the center test decides which, and for a shape many pixels wide the wrong calls all sit in a one-pixel band along the edge where nobody's looking closely. Thin shapes are where nobody can look anywhere else: the whole shape is the edge. Type is thin. So are the tiger's whiskers, and every hairline in every chart. The fix everyone reaches for first is chapter 2's supersampler — ask sixty-four times per pixel instead of once — and it works, and it's the reference this chapter checks against, and it's slow and still only approximate. There's a better way, and it's not a refinement of sampling. It stops sampling.

Here's the whole idea in one sentence. Coverage is an area, area is an integral, and the integral of a polygon can be computed edge by edge instead of point by point. Walk each edge once. As it passes through a cell, it splits that cell into a piece on its left and a piece on its right, and you record how much area it put on the left and how much height it carries to everything further right. Do that for every edge, then sweep each row of cells from left to right adding up what came before. What falls out is the exact covered fraction of every pixel, and because it's exact, the total lit area of a filled polygon comes out equal to the polygon's real area, to the last decimal. The supersampler can't say that. This chapter can.

§ 7.1 Two numbers per cell

Chapter 2's buffer stored one number per pixel: its coverage. This chapter builds coverage up out of edge crossings, and to do that it needs a scratch buffer that holds two numbers per cell while the edges are being deposited. Call them area and cover. Area is what the cell itself has accumulated: the signed area an edge left inside this one cell. Cover is the signed height that this cell hands to every cell on its right — because an edge that passes up through a cell doesn't only affect that cell, it changes whether everything to the right of it is inside or outside. The accumulator is a grid of those pairs, all zero, and area_at and cover_at read them back.

add_cell(acc, x, row, area, cover) is the one primitive that writes to the buffer, and it adds rather than overwrites, because two edges of the same shape can cross the same cell and both have a say. Two of its cases are about the edge of the buffer, and they're the kind of thing that's invisible until a shape hangs off the side of the canvas and you get a stripe of garbage down column zero. A deposit meant for a cell left of the buffer doesn't vanish: everything in that row is to the right of it, so it folds onto column 0 and counts as pure cover. Column 0 is entirely to the piece's right, so its area is the whole cover: the incoming area argument is thrown away and replaced by the cover, not added to it — the scenario deposits (0.3, 0.7) off the left and reads 0.7 back, not 0.3 and not 1.0. A deposit right of the buffer really does vanish, because there's nothing to its right to carry into. State both now and the clipping in §7.3 comes out right without a special case.

§ 7.2 One edge, one row

Now the geometry, at the smallest scale: a straight edge crossing a single row of cells. Clip the edge to the row's top and bottom and you get a little segment running from some x0 at the top to some x1 at the bottom, spanning a signed height — call it the piece. accumulate_row(acc, row, x0, x1, height) deposits it. If the piece stays inside one cell, that cell gets the whole height as its cover, and its area is the height weighted by how far the piece sits from the cell's right edge — a piece hugging the left of the cell shadows almost the entire cell, so its area is almost the full height; a piece hugging the right shadows almost nothing.

That weighting is the whole trick, and it's worth seeing why it's exact rather than close. The shaded region on the left of the edge is a trapezoid, and the area of a trapezoid is its height times its width at the halfway line. The edge is straight, so its x at the vertical midpoint of the piece is the average of x0 and x1, and the width to the left of it is one minus that. No sampling, no subdivision, no error term — a single multiply gives the true area under a straight edge. That's the sense in which this rasterizer is exact where the supersampler is a good guess.

When the piece spans more than one cell, cut it at the cell boundaries and hand each cell its slice: the height is shared out in proportion to the width the piece has in that cell, and each slice uses the same midpoint rule inside its own cell. The scenarios below pin a piece that stays in one cell, a slanted one that leans its area left, a wide one split three ways, and a piece carrying a negative height — because an edge coming back down the shape deposits the same shape of numbers with the sign flipped, and §7.4 leans on that.

§ 7.3 Walking the edge down its rows

An edge usually crosses more than one row, so accumulate(acc, a, b) walks it down, clipping it to each row it touches and handing the piece to accumulate_row. The sign is the point of the whole thing. An edge heading up the canvas carries a positive height; heading down, negative. Deposit a shape's edges that way and the left-to-right running sum in the next section comes out as chapter 5's winding number, cell for cell, fractional at the edges and whole in the middle. Watch one detail if you go looking: this is the reverse of the per-edge sign chapter 6 gave an edge in its direction field, because chapter 6 counted crossings from the point's right and this accumulates cover from its left. The two meet at the same total, which is why the running sum matches winding_at and not chapter 6's span walk. And since the fill takes the absolute value in the end, a shape drawn clockwise or counter-clockwise fills the same — the sign only has to be consistent within a shape, and this makes it so.

Three degenerate cases live in this function, and each is a real bug in an implementation that skips it. A horizontal edge has zero height and deposits nothing — same as chapter 6's dropped horizontal, for the same reason, and a bonus reason: its slope would divide by zero. An edge that runs off the top or bottom of the buffer contributes only the rows it genuinely crosses; clipping the row range to the buffer is what keeps a tall shape from walking off the end of the array. And an edge entirely left of the buffer covers the whole of every row it spans, through the column-0 folding from §7.1, while one entirely right deposits nothing. Those last two are why a shape three times the size of the canvas still fills the canvas solid instead of striping it.

§ 7.4 The running sum

Every edge is in the buffer now, each cell holding its own area and the cover it passes right. resolve(acc, rule) turns that into coverage in one sweep per row. Start at the left with a running total of zero. At each cell, the winding number there is the running total so far — the cover of everything to its left — plus this cell's own area. Then fold this cell's cover into the running total and step right. One addition per cell, one row at a time, and the prefix sum reconstructs the exact winding at every pixel from the sparse marks the edges left.

The winding number can land anywhere now, not only on whole numbers, so the fill rule has to cope with fractions. apply_rule does it. For "nonzero", coverage is the winding's absolute value capped at 1: a cell half-swept by one edge reads 0.5, a cell swept fully once or twice reads 1. For "evenodd", it's a triangle wave — the absolute winding folded back and forth between 0 and 1, so a fractional winding of 1.25 reads 0.75 and a winding of 2 reads 0, which is the even-odd hole appearing exactly at the doubly-wound region. Both rules agree with chapter 5 on whole windings and interpolate sensibly between them, which is all a soft edge needs.

§ 7.5 The fill, and how you know it's right

Assembling it is anticlimactic, which is the nicest thing you can say about a rasterizer. fill_path(p, rule, w, h) makes an accumulator, deposits every edge of the path with accumulate, and calls resolve. That's the fill from here to the end of the book. It replaces chapter 6's fill_path_aliased wholesale — the sweep was a fine renderer and a good way to understand spans, but this one gives back the soft edges that one threw away, at about the same speed. Chapter 6's fill stays in the drawer as the honest jagged reference and as a thing to diff against.

fill_path(p, rule, w, h):
  acc ← accumulator(w, h)
  for (a, b) in edges(p):
    accumulate(acc, a, b)
  return resolve(acc, rule)

How do you know a soft edge is correct and not merely soft? A blurry wrong answer is still wrong, and it's harder to spot than a jagged one. The test that settles it is beautiful: for a simple polygon, the total ink of the fill — every pixel's coverage summed — equals the polygon's exact area. Coverage is area shared out among pixels, so if the rasterizer is exact, the shares add back to the whole. A rectangle from x = 1.5 to 4.75 and y = 2 to 5 has area 3.25 × 3 = 9.75, and the fill's ink is 9.75, not 9.7-something. A twelve-sided polygon standing in for a circle has ink equal to its shoelace area to six decimals; chapter 2's supersampler gets the same shape visibly right but its ink is off in the third decimal, because sixty-four samples can't resolve a slanted edge exactly and this can. polygon_area(p) is the shoelace formula, in the reference so the scenarios can hold the two against each other.

Reading these tests

The exact-scalar tier is doing real work here. A cell reads 0.5 or 0.75 to the full tolerance because a straight edge's area is a rational number, not a sampled estimate — so these are pinned as equalities, not with ±. The star, whose corners come from sin and cos, carries a small ± the way chapter 5's star crossings did. And the payoff renders are diffed against a reference image at max_channel_difference ≤ 1, one byte, the golden-image tier from chapter 1.

The rest of the scenarios pin the corners. Where a shape's edges land on the sample grid — an axis-aligned rectangle, that particular triangle — the fill and chapter 2's supersampler agree to the bit, so max_coverage_difference between them is exactly 0, and you get to check the exact renderer against the slow trusted one and watch them match. Where they part, on a slanted edge, they part because the supersampler is quantizing to sixty-fourths and the fill isn't; the fill and chapter 6's aliased sweep agree on every fully-covered and fully-empty pixel and disagree only in the soft band, where the aliased one says 1 and the exact one says 0.75. A doubled square reads solid under nonzero and hollow under even-odd, the winding-2 test from chapter 5 now carried by fractional coverage. And the star, both rules, comes out with the same holes chapter 5 argued about, now with edges you'd be happy to ship.

The trap

The coverage this chapter computes is exact — but exact under a box filter, and exact for one shape considered alone. Both caveats are free until chapter 9, and then neither is. Box filter: the fill weights every part of a pixel equally, which is what "area" means, and it's what almost everyone ships; a fancier filter would weight the middle of the pixel more, and this method can't do that without giving up its speed. Considered alone: two shapes that share an edge each compute their own honest coverage of the boundary pixels, and if you then add the two coverages you count that shared edge twice and get a bright seam down the join. Every renderer on earth has this seam and almost none of them mention it. It's not a bug in this chapter — the coverage is right for each shape — it's what happens when you forget that coverage was only ever the answer to a question about one shape. Chapter 9 is where you learn to composite through coverage instead of adding it, and the seam is the first thing it fixes.

In the GUI

Photoshop's type layers carry an anti-aliasing menu — None, Sharp, Crisp, Strong, Smooth. None is chapter 6. The other four are different filters laid over exactly the coverage this chapter computes, trading a little blur for a little edge contrast. The math underneath every one of them is the pass you wrote here.

§ 7.6 Putting it together

Time to make something the last chapter couldn't. Chapter 6's spiral of stars, rerun through fill_path, is the same picture with the staircases sanded off — a fair before-and-after, and a one-line change. But the payoff wants thin things, because thin things are where this chapter wins by a mile. So: a sunburst. Seventy-two rays from the center, each a sliver a couple of degrees wide, in three inks; punch a disc out of the middle by painting the paper colour back through a filled circle; and drop a five-pointed star into the hole, cut with the even-odd rule so its pentagram shows. Every one of those slivers is a needle from Figure 7.1, and every one comes out clean.

    move_to(p, point(240, 240))
    line_to(p, point(240 + 232·cos(a − half), 240 + 232·sin(a − half)))
    line_to(p, point(240 + 232·cos(a + half), 240 + 232·sin(a + half)))
    close(p)
  return p

sunburst():
  c ← canvas(480, 480);  fill(c, PAPER)
  for i in 0..2:                                // three inks, every third ray
    paint_through(c, fill_path(rays(i), "nonzero", 480, 480), INKS[i])
  disc ← circle_path(240, 240, 78, 180)
  paint_through(c, fill_path(disc, "nonzero", 480, 480), PAPER)   // paper punches the hole
  m ← translation(240, 240) * scaling(64, 64)
  paint_through(c, fill_path(transform_path(unit_star(), m), "evenodd", 480, 480), PALE)
  return c

plate_07():
  return sunburst()

The rays, the disc and the star are each a path filled to a coverage buffer and painted through with one colour, exactly as chapter 2 set up: coverage says where, the colour says what, and the paper-coloured disc is the same operation used to take paint away instead of add it. The scenarios pin one pixel of each ink out at the rim, a pale pixel on a star arm, the paper hole at the dead centre where the pentagram opens, and a corner of bare paper — then diff the whole 480-by-480 image against the reference. The needle render and the smooth spiral get pinned the same way, and the needle one carries the number that started the chapter: chapter 6's fill lights 268 pixels' worth of ink, this chapter's lights the needles' true area, and the difference is every tip that used to fall through the grid.

The four smaller renders the scenarios pin are all compositions of shapes you already have, so here they are in full — the needles opener from the top of the chapter, the soft square from §7.5, chapter 5's star filled exactly, and chapter 6's spiral rerun through fill_path:

Every ray in that burst is thinner at its root than a pixel, and none of them break. That's the chapter. From here on, coverage is soft and it's exact, which means the next question isn't "where is the shape" — you can answer that to the last decimal now — but "what colour goes through the coverage, and what happens where two of them meet." That's the seam from the trap, and it's chapter 9. First, though, chapter 8 teaches the shapes to curve, because so far every path has been straight lines, and the tiger is not made of straight lines.
