Chapter Twenty-Two

Boolean Path Operations

You've been able to draw a donut since chapter 5: two circles in one path, and the fill rule punches the hole. Now somebody wants the donut itself. They want its outline as a path, to stroke it, save it, or cut a keyhole out of it. They want to take a letter g, lay a star over it, and keep only the parts where exactly one of them is. No earlier chapter can do that, because every earlier chapter answered its questions one pixel at a time, and this question's answer is a new path, with vertices nobody wrote down. This chapter finds them. It's the hardest code in the book, for a reason that has nothing to do with geometry.

The idea fits in a paragraph. Take every edge of both paths. Wherever two edges cross, cut both of them there, and keep cutting until no two edges cross anywhere. Now every piece has the same thing on each of its sides all the way along, so ask of each piece, once, whether it's inside each path on its left and on its right. Chapter 5's winding number answers that in one line, which is the reward for having chosen it. Keep the pieces where the result is inside on one side and outside on the other. Link the kept pieces end to end, and you have the outline.

The difficulty is that the paragraph assumes you can tell whether two edges cross, whether a point is on a line, and whether two points are the same point. With floating point you can't, not reliably, and a boolean operation asks those questions tens of thousands of times near exactly the configurations where the answer is borderline: shared vertices, edges that run along each other, a corner resting on an edge. Get one of them wrong and the result isn't a little off. It's a path that crosses itself, a sliver a billionth of a pixel wide, or a contour that never closes. So this chapter starts by making every one of those questions exact, and pays for it with a grid.

§ 22.1 A grid

Snap every coordinate to a grid of 1/256 of a pixel and store it as a whole number of grid units: grid(v) is floor(v × 256 + 0.5), halves up, like every rounding in this book. A point 3.14159 pixels from the origin is 804 units from it. Nobody can see a 256th of a pixel, and chapter 21 already told you that production rasterizers snap to exactly this grid for their own reasons.

What the grid buys is orient(a, b, c), chapter 4's cross product of b − a and c − a, computed without error. It's positive when c is clockwise of the line from a to b on screen, negative when it's counterclockwise and 0 when the three are collinear, and on whole numbers that 0 is a real 0. Keep every coordinate within 1024 pixels of the origin, 262,144 units, and every difference is below 219 and every product below 240, which a double holds exactly with thirteen bits to spare. So this works even in a language whose only number is a double. At the far corners of the grid, orient can tell you that a point is one unit off a line half a million units long, and be right.

The last thing the section needs is an order. The sweeps later in the chapter walk down the canvas, and they need to know which of two points comes first even when they're on the same row. lex_less(p, q) puts them top to bottom and then left to right. It's the same idea as chapter 5's half-open rule, taken one step further: chapter 5 broke ties between a row and an edge's endpoint by treating the top end as in and the bottom end as out; this breaks ties between two points on the same row by x.

§ 22.2 Where two segments meet

From here on a path is a list of segments. A seg has two grid points, stored in the sweep's order, lo first. It also has two small integers, wa and wb: how much it adds to the winding number of path A and of path B. An edge of A that ran from lo to hi is +1 for A, one that ran the other way is −1, and either is 0 for B. That's the edge's direction, which chapter 5 needed to count windings, kept as a number because storing the ends in order threw the direction away. path_segments makes one of these for every edge of a path, and drops any edge whose ends snap to the same point.

meet(s, t) says how two segments meet and where each has to be cut. There are four ways, drawn in Figure 22.1, and every test that tells them apart is an orient. They cross when each one's ends are strictly on opposite sides of the other's line. One touches the other when an end of it lies strictly inside the other, which has to be cut there. They overlap when they're collinear and share more than a point, and then each is cut at the other's ends that lie inside it. And they meet at an end when they share an endpoint and nothing else, which needs no cutting at all.

A crossing is the one case that makes a new point, and the new point isn't on the grid. The exact crossing of segments from a to b and from c to d is a + (b − a) × α / β, with β = cross(b − a, d − c) and α = cross(c − a, d − c). Round each coordinate of that to the nearest grid point, halves up. Do it in whole numbers too: each coordinate is N / β for a whole number N, and rounding a fraction is (2N + β) div 2β, with div rounding down toward −∞. N gets as big as 259, which is the one place in the chapter that needs 64-bit integers. When the rounded point lands on an end of one of the segments, that segment isn't cut, and the other one is cut at its end.

§ 22.3 Splitting until nothing crosses

To split a list of segments, meet every pair, collect every segment's cut points, and cut. Order the cut points along the segment by how far they are along it, dot(q − lo, hi − lo), and a segment with two cut points becomes three pieces, each keeping its parent's windings. That's find_splits with the method "brute". It meets n(n − 1)/2 pairs, which is fine for two squares and will stop being fine in § 22.6.

Two things can go wrong, and both are fixed by the same loop. First, cutting can make two identical segments, from two paths that shared an edge or a path that doubled back on itself. merge_segments makes them one and adds their windings up, and a segment whose windings add to nothing is dropped, because it no longer changes anybody's winding number: a spike that goes out and comes back vanishes here. Second, rounding a crossing moves it, by up to half a grid unit in each direction, and a cut segment isn't straight any more. It turns, very slightly, at the rounded point. A segment that turns can hit something it missed.

So split_segments goes round: merge, find, and if anything needs cutting, cut and go again, until a pass finds nothing. Most inputs take two passes, one that cuts and one that proves there's nothing left. The one in Figure 22.2 takes three. There's a theory, called snap rounding, that routes every segment through the grid cells it passes near and so never needs a second pass; the loop is simpler to write, and every drawing in this chapter settles in two passes; it took a search to find three segments that need a third.

§ 22.4 Which side is inside

Now nothing crosses. Every segment has one region on its left and one on its right all the way along, and the winding number of each path is constant across each region. So ask once per segment, at its midpoint. And chapter 5 already wrote the question: a ray from the point toward +x, adding up the edges it crosses, each with its direction.

Two small changes turn winding_at into winding_beside. The half-open rule compares points with lex_less instead of heights alone, so that segment f counts for the midpoint m when f.lo is at or before m and m is before f.hi. That settles which of two segments meeting at m's height counts without an epsilon, as the tie-breaking scenario shows. And chapter 5's two cases, down-going edges with the point on their left and up-going edges with the point on their right, become one: with every segment stored top to bottom, f counts when orient(f.lo, f.hi, m) > 0, and what it adds is its own windings, positive for an edge that ran downward and negative for one that ran up. Sum that over every segment but the one you're asking about, and you have the winding numbers of A and B on one side of it, the side where it doesn't count. Add the segment's own windings and you have the other side, its left, or below it when it's horizontal.

Then classification really is a lookup. inside_rule turns each winding number into in or out under that path's own fill rule, op_inside combines the two answers, and a segment is on the boundary of the result exactly when the answer differs between its sides. Keep it, and point it so that the inside is on your right as you travel along it: lo to hi when the inside is on the side where it counts, hi to lo when it isn't. Right-hand inside is clockwise on screen, the same direction chapter 5 called positive, so the result will have winding 1 inside and 0 outside.

§ 22.5 Stitching

The kept segments are a pile of arrows, and every vertex has as many arrows leaving it as arriving. stitch links them into loops: start with the first unused arrow in the sweep's order, follow it, and at each vertex take an unused arrow leaving it until you're back where you started. Most vertices have one way out. The ones with two are where the result touches itself at a point, two squares meeting at a corner or a star's arm resting on the rim of a circle, and there you take the arrow that turns furthest right. With the inside on your right, the sharpest right turn follows the edge of the region you're walking around instead of stepping into the next one, and two squares that touch at a corner stay two squares.

"Furthest right" is exact too. Face back the way you came; the arrow you want is the first one you meet turning counterclockwise from there. Two cross products and a dot product put any two directions in that order: which half-turn each is in, then which comes first within a half-turn.

Then tidy each loop so that equal results are equal paths. Splitting left vertices where the outline runs straight on, so drop every vertex that's collinear with its two neighbours. Start each contour at its topmost vertex, the leftmost if two tie, and sort the contours. Now a result can be compared point for point, which is what makes the scenarios below possible: a shape united with itself is exactly the shape, and a shape minus itself is exactly nothing.

combine is the whole pipeline, and simplify is combine with an empty second path. That's more useful than it sounds: it takes one path's fill rule and its self-crossings and turns them into a plain outline, which is how chapter 5's star becomes a ten-sided polygon and how a glyph with overlapping contours becomes one you can offset.

§ 22.6 A faster way to find crossings

Everything so far is correct, and nearly all of the time goes into meeting pairs of segments that are nowhere near each other. The chapter's benchmark is struck_line(1): the word Pathfinder in Roboto at 120 pixels, 787 segments once it's flattened and snapped, struck through by fourteen slanting bars. Its first pass meets 309,291 pairs to find 184 places that need cutting.

Two segments can only meet if they share a row. So sort them by their tops and sweep down the canvas with an active list, chapter 6's idea again: when a segment's top arrives, drop every active segment that has already ended, meet the new one against what's left, and add it. This is a simplified Bentley–Ottmann: the sweep, without the order that makes the real one fast. It finds exactly the same cuts, because two segments that meet are both active when the second of them arrives, and on the benchmark it meets 34,326 pairs, a ninth as many.

§ 22.7 Bentley–Ottmann

Keep the active list in order, left to right along the sweep line, and call it the status. A little above the point where two segments cross, nothing lies between them, so at some moment before they cross they're next to each other in the status, and that's the moment to test them. So a new segment is tested against its two neighbours only. When one leaves, its two neighbours become neighbours and are tested. And when two cross, they swap places, which gives each of them a new neighbour to test. The crossings can't be known in advance, so they go into the same queue as the segment ends, as events, handled in the sweep's order. That's Bentley and Ottmann's algorithm, from 1979, and the textbook version handles every degenerate case in one step: at each event, find every segment passing through the point at once.

handle(P):                                  // every lo and hi is an event at the start
  st.events ← st.events + 1
  block ← the status segments with orient(lo, hi, P) = 0      // binary search
  L ← those of block whose hi is P;  C ← the rest of block
  for each s of C: split s at P rounded to the grid
  new ← U(P) and C, left to right as they leave P
  replace block by new in the status
  if new is empty: test(left of the gap, right of the gap)
  else: test(left of new, first of new);  test(last of new, right of new)

test(s, t):                                  // only when both exist
  st.tests ← st.tests + 1
  if s and t cross, and the exact crossing X comes after P:
    add X to the events, unless it's already there

Two details decide whether it works. The first is the order in which the new block leaves P: segment s is left of t below P when cross(t.hi − t.lo, s.hi − s.lo) > 0, exact, and a horizontal segment, which the sweep's order makes run from left to right, comes last. The second is that the events must be exact. A crossing isn't a grid point, it's a fraction, (X/D, Y/D), and if you store it as two doubles the status will sooner or later be out of order by a rounding error, and a Bentley–Ottmann with a misordered status doesn't find a slightly wrong answer. It stops looking at the pairs that cross. Keep the numerators and the denominator as whole numbers: testing a segment against an event is dx × (Y − D × lo.y) − dy × (X − D × lo.x), about 80 bits, and putting two events in order compares Y1 × D2 with Y2 × D1, about 100. Python's integers don't mind. Java has BigInteger, Rust and C# have 128-bit integers, and JavaScript has BigInt. The grid's rounded point is only used for the cut, never for the order.

§ 22.8 The hard cases

The trap

Numerical robustness is the entire difficulty, and floating point fails it on the first case you try. The crossing of the segments from (2.6, 2.3) to (10, 4.7) and from (8.4, 4.8) to (6.4, 1.5), computed in doubles, is a point that lies on neither of them: the cross products are −3.6 × 10−15 and 3.3 × 10−15. So the next test, "is this point on that segment?", says no, and you add an epsilon. Now a point within the epsilon of a segment is on it, and two points within the epsilon of each other are the same point, and that isn't transitive: A is the same as B, B is the same as C, A isn't the same as C. There's always an input that puts three points in a row like that, and then the result has a contour that doesn't close, or a sliver of width 10−9 with two crossings at each end. Every serious polygon clipper ends up where this chapter started. Clipper, the library a great deal of vector software leans on for this, works in 64-bit integers, rounds its crossings to whole numbers, and multiplies in 128 bits where 64 run out. The grid has its own price, and it's better to know it than to meet it. Every crossing moves by up to 1/512 of a pixel. A shape thinner than the grid disappears. Rounding a crossing can make a new one, which is why split_segments loops. And every coordinate has to fit in the grid's range.

The scenarios in this section are the cases that break boolean code written without a grid: edges that coincide completely or in part, shapes that touch at one point, a corner resting on an edge, a spike, and a shape thinner than the grid. Each has an exact answer here, and it's the answer you'd draw by hand.

§ 22.9 Putting it together

The whole of combine, with any of the three finders inside find_splits. It's twenty lines, and every line of it has a scenario.

combine(a, rule_a, b, rule_b, op):
  segs ← merge_segments(path_segments(a, "a") + path_segments(b, "b"))
  loop:
    splits ← find_splits(segs, method, st)
    if every list in splits is empty: stop
    segs ← merge_segments(each segment cut at its splits, in order along it)
  kept ← []
  for each segment e of segs:
    m ← e.lo + e.hi;  (wa, wb) ← (0, 0)                  // the midpoint, doubled
    for each other segment f:
      if 2 f.lo ≤ m < 2 f.hi in the sweep's order and orient(2 f.lo, 2 f.hi, m) > 0:
        (wa, wb) ← (wa + f.wa, wb + f.wb)
    out ← op_inside(op, inside_rule(wa, rule_a), inside_rule(wb, rule_b))
    in  ← op_inside(op, inside_rule(wa + e.wa, rule_a), inside_rule(wb + e.wb, rule_b))
    if out ≠ in: kept ← kept + [(e.lo, e.hi) if in else (e.hi, e.lo)]
  for each contour of stitch(kept):
    a closed subpath through its points, each coordinate ÷ 256

The loop over every other segment makes the classification n2 again. The reference does it with a sweep over the midpoints, keeping the segments whose range contains the current one, which is the active list for the third time in this chapter; the answer is the same either way.

The plate puts it to work on chapter 16's g and chapter 5's star, the star filled even-odd so that its middle is a hole, which is the most awkward thing to hand an operation that has to respect both fill rules. Union, intersection, difference and exclusive-or, left to right. The magenta outline over each fill is the result path itself, and the fill is chapter 7 filling that path. Nothing here is a pixel operation. The result is still a path, and you could draw it a thousand times bigger and get a crisp edge.

And because a boolean operation takes paths and answers a path, it composes. The seal is sixty-one of them, with nothing drawn any other way: forty little circles united with a big one to scallop the rim, a smaller circle cut out of the middle, a band added across it, the word BOOLEAN exclusive-or'ed through the band, the star exclusive-or'ed through the top of the ring, and a rosette of sixteen circles, each exclusive-or'ed into the last, whose checkerboard comes from nothing but overlapping circles and the rule that twice inside is outside.
