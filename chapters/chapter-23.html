Chapter Twenty-Three

Distance Fields

Chapter 13 needed about 120 lines to stroke a path. Chapter 14 needed 130 more to do it properly for curves, and chapter 22 needed 260 to cut one path with another, most of them spent on making floating point tell the truth. Here are all three again: |d| − w/2, d − r and max(min(a, b), −max(a, b)). That's the stroke, the offset and the exclusive-or, one line each, and they work on any shape at all. The catch is the letter d. This chapter stores, at every pixel, the distance to the nearest edge instead of how much of the pixel is covered, and a startling amount of the book turns into arithmetic on that one number. It isn't free, and by the end you'll know exactly what it costs.

A signed distance field gives every point the distance to the nearest point of a shape's edge, negative inside and positive outside. The edge is where the field is 0, so drawing the shape is asking which pixels have a field near 0, and a field is a different answer to the question every chapter since chapter 2 has asked: what is this shape, as numbers? Coverage answers "how much of this pixel". A field answers "how far to the edge", and the second answer turns out to know things about the neighbourhood that the first never did.

§ 23.1 Exact fields for primitives

A circle's field is the distance to its center less its radius. A segment's field is the distance to its nearest point, found by projecting onto it and clamping to its ends; it has no sign, because a segment has no inside. A box's is two lines that are worth staring at until they make sense: measure how far outside each pair of sides the point is, qx and qy; the length of the positive parts is the distance when you're outside, and the larger of the two is the (negative) distance when you're inside, the nearest side. Round the box by shrinking it by r and subtracting r. That's the first sign of what's coming: rounding every corner of a shape is subtracting a number.

A polygon is the least distance to any of its edges, which is the size; and the sign is chapter 5's winding number, under whichever fill rule you like. That's the one place a field needs the whole path at once, and it's the one place a field can get the wrong answer from a perfectly good path, which § 23.3 finds.

§ 23.2 Fields for curves

The distance to a curve is the distance to its nearest point, and the nearest point B(t) is where the line from p meets the curve at right angles: (B(t) − p) · B′(t) = 0. For a quadratic, B has degree two and B′ degree one, so that's a cubic in t, and cubics have a formula. Solve it, keep the roots between 0 and 1, add the two ends, and the nearest of those points is the answer, exactly. The formula has two branches, one real root or three, and the scenarios pin both, along with the cases where the leading coefficients vanish because the curve is a straight line in disguise, which is what every TrueType line segment is.

For a cubic the same condition has degree five, and quintics don't have a formula. So guess and improve: nine seeds along the curve, eight steps of Newton's method from each, and the nearest result. And the two ends, on their own, because Newton's method starting at an end can walk away from it toward a stationary point inside the curve, while the end was the answer all along. The scenario with the point at (70, 80) is that case; a version without the ends says 59.7 where the answer is 50.

Both are testable against a brute-force search, and the test is where this section earned its keep. The first ground truth was chapter 14's distance_to_curve, which samples the curve at 65 points and refines around the best one. Against two hundred random cubics it disagreed with Newton twice, by up to a tenth of a pixel. The Newton answer was right: on a cubic that loops back past the point, the best sample was near the wrong stretch of the curve, and chapter 14 refined the wrong minimum. That search is fine for what chapter 14 used it for, curves that don't loop. The brute force here refines around every sample that's closer than its neighbours, and against it both fields agree with it to within 10−11 over ten thousand points.

§ 23.3 Render it

A field becomes coverage in one line: clamp(0.5 − d, 0, 1). A pixel whose center is on the edge is half covered, and the coverage ramps to nothing half a pixel outside and to everything half a pixel in. That's the whole rasterizer. Now diff it against chapter 7's exact one and measure where it's wrong, because it is.

Along an edge that runs with the pixels, it's exact: a pixel 0.2 inside the edge of an axis-aligned box is 0.7 covered, and that's what both say. Along a slanted edge, it's a little off, because a pixel is a square and the coverage of a square by a half-plane isn't a straight ramp: at 45° the true ramp is gentler at its ends, and the linear one can be off by 0.043; the scenario's pixel is off by 0.035. At a corner it's a long way off. The field measures to the corner point itself, so its zero line goes round the corner instead of into it, and at the tips of the star the error is nearly half a pixel's worth of ink.

And inside the star there's an error that isn't in any textbook, because it's this book's own doing. The star is five crossing edges filled nonzero, so the pentagon in the middle is inside and the edges running across it aren't the shape's edge any more. The fill rule knows that; the field doesn't. It measures to every edge, finds the buried ones half a pixel away, and draws a faint seam along each. The fix is chapter 22: simplify the path first, and the field measures to the real outline. The total error drops from 44.8 pixels' worth of ink to 9.8, and what's left is the points.

§ 23.4 Chapters 13, 14 and 22 for free

Grow a shape by r: every point that was r or closer to it is now in it, and its field is d − r. A stroke w wide is every point within w/2 of the edge, |d| − w/2, with round joins and round caps because distance is round. A union is everything inside either, and inside either means the smaller field is negative: min(a, b). Intersection is max, difference is max(a, −b), and exclusive-or is inside one and not both. Each is one line, applied at every pixel, and none of them has a special case.

Figure 23.3 draws three earlier chapters' work both ways, the top row by path and the bottom by field. The star's stroke by field and by chapter 13's stroker, with round joins, differ by at most 0.41 at any pixel and by 4.4 pixels of ink in 5,906; the difference is mostly chapter 13's arcs, which are sixteen-sided polygons, against the field's true circles. The curve's stroke differs from chapter 14's by at most 0.08. The exclusive-or differs from chapter 22's plate by at most 0.41, at the corners, where the field rounds and the path doesn't.

That's the honest comparison of code length, too. The field versions need the fields, which are the thirty lines of § 23.1 and the sixty of § 23.2, and then each operation is one line. The path versions need 120, 130 and 260 lines, and give you a path at the end, which you can stroke again, offset exactly, save as SVG and scale to a billboard. The field gives you a grid of numbers at one resolution. That trade is the chapter.

§ 23.5 Something paths can't do

min(a, b) has a crease wherever a = b, and the crease is the sharp corner where two shapes meet. Replace it with a function that agrees with min when a and b are far apart and dips a little below both when they're close, and the crease fills in. The polynomial smooth minimum dips by at most k/4, only where |a − b| < k, and the result is a fillet whose size k controls. It's a shape with no path expression: not a circular arc, not any curve chapter 8 can write, but a blend that sweeps smoothly out of one outline and into the other.

In the GUI

Photoshop's layer styles, Stroke, Outer Glow, Drop Shadow and its Spread slider, are at heart thresholds and falloffs on a distance field of the layer's pixels, which is why they're instant and why their corners come out round. Illustrator's Offset Path is the exact version, on the path, and it's noticeably slower.

§ 23.6 The distance transform

Every field so far came from a path. A distance transform makes one from pixels: a bitmap, a coverage buffer, a scan of a signature. Decide which pixels are on, and find, for every pixel, the squared distance to the nearest center that's on. Trying every pair is exact and takes n2 steps; Felzenszwalb and Huttenlocher's transform is exact and takes n.

It works one line at a time, and it's the prettiest trick in the chapter. In one dimension, each pixel p offers every q the distance (q − p)² + f[p], a parabola with its foot at height f[p]; the answer at q is the lowest parabola there. The lower envelope of a row of parabolas can be built left to right, because each new one either takes over from some point on or doesn't matter, and z records where each takes over. In two dimensions, do every column, then every row of what the columns gave you, because a squared distance is a squared distance down plus a squared distance across.

edt_1d(f):                                   // d[q] = min over p of (q − p)² + f[p]
  k ← 0;  v[0] ← 0;  z[0] ← −∞;  z[1] ← +∞
  for q from 1 to n − 1:
    s ← ((f[q] + q²) − (f[v[k]] + v[k]²)) / (2q − 2v[k])      // where q's parabola crosses v[k]'s
    while s ≤ z[k]:                          // v[k] never gets to be lowest
      k ← k − 1
      s ← ((f[q] + q²) − (f[v[k]] + v[k]²)) / (2q − 2v[k])
    k ← k + 1;  v[k] ← q;  z[k] ← s;  z[k + 1] ← +∞
  k ← 0
  for q from 0 to n − 1:
    while z[k + 1] < q: k ← k + 1
    d[q] ← (q − v[k])² + f[v[k]]

Use w² + h² for "far", not infinity: infinity minus infinity is nobody's friend, and a finite far value keeps every number a whole number. The only division's result is compared and never stored, and two different fractions with denominators this small are always farther apart than a double's rounding, so the answer is exact, and the scenario checks it against trying every pixel. Then a signed field from any coverage buffer is two transforms, one to the nearest pixel that's on and one to the nearest that's off, less half a pixel each, which puts the edge between the centers.

§ 23.7 Glyph atlases

Here is why distance fields are everywhere. Bake a glyph's field once, small, into a texture: chapter 17's bitmap box, grown by a few texels, with the distance at every texel center. Then draw it at any size by sampling that texture bilinearly, as chapter 11 does, and turning the interpolated distance into coverage. The distance interpolates far better than coverage does, because a field is smooth where coverage is a cliff, so a glyph baked at 16 pixels can be drawn at 320 and still have edges, where chapter 17's bitmap magnified would be a pile of bricks. Most game engines draw their text this way.

Look at the corners, though. Bilinear interpolation of one number can only draw zero lines that are smooth at the scale of a texel, and a corner isn't. At twenty times, the E's corners are round and its arms end in knobs. The cure is Chlumsky's multi-channel field, and it's a lovely piece of reasoning. Give each edge of the outline a colour, a set of two of the three channels, so that at every corner the two edges that meet share exactly one channel. In each channel store the distance to the nearest edge that carries it, and past an edge's ends, the distance to the line the edge would carry on along, its pseudo-distance. Now near a corner each of the two private channels sees only its own edge, extended straight past the corner, and the median of the three channels is the smaller of two half-planes: a sharp corner, recovered from three smooth numbers.

The colouring rule is small. Find the corners, where the curve's direction turns by more than about eight degrees. With no corners, every edge is white, all three channels, and the field is an ordinary one. With one corner, a teardrop, split the contour into three runs from the corner and colour them cyan, white and magenta. With more, alternate cyan and magenta at every corner, and if the count is odd make the last run yellow so that it doesn't meet the first in two channels. Any two neighbouring runs then share exactly one channel.

It has one real limit, and the figure shows it. The k baked at 16 pixels has strokes about one and a half texels wide, and there the channels of the two sides of a stroke fight over the same texels: its diagonals come out notched. Baked at 32, three texels to a stroke, it's clean. Multi-channel fields need about two texels across the thinnest stroke, which is why atlases for them are baked at 32 or 48 pixels, not 16. And the median is only a distance near the edge. Far from it the extended lines of other edges make false near-misses, so effects that reach away from the edge, a glow or a shadow, read a fourth channel that holds the ordinary distance. That's bake_mtsdf, and the title below uses it.

One edge case before you bake a whole string: a space has no outline, so there's no edge for any channel to be near. Every channel with nothing to measure, whether a whole blank glyph or a colour no edge happens to carry, is spread at every texel, as far out as the clamp allows. Leave that out and the first space in a title is a crash, or a min over nothing, depending on your language's taste in failure.

§ 23.8 Fields compose approximately

The trap

min(a, b) is not the signed distance field of the union. It's right outside: the nearest edge of the union from outside is the nearer of the two shapes' nearest edges. It's wrong inside, because the nearest edge of each shape may be buried inside the other. Two circles of radius 40 whose centers are 50 apart make a peanut, and at the middle of its waist min says the edge is 15 away, the depth inside each circle; the real edge is the notch where the circles cross, 31.2 away. The fill doesn't care, because the sign is right everywhere, so the error is invisible until you offset or stroke the result. Then it's very visible: shrink the peanut by 20 through min and it falls into two separate discs; shrink the true field of chapter 22's union by 20 and it stays one peanut with a waist. Every operation in § 23.4 has this problem: its answer is a field whose zero line is right and whose values aren't distances any more, so the second operation is working from bad data. Distance fields compose approximately. Paths compose exactly. That trade is the whole chapter.

§ 23.9 Putting it together

The heart of the multi-channel bake is one texel, and it fits in a screen. Everything above it is the box and the colouring; everything after is draw_baked taking a median.

msdf_texel(p, edges, spread):                // edges: (curve, mask) pairs
  for each channel ch of RED, GREEN, BLUE:
    best ← none
    for each (c, mask) of edges with mask and ch:
      d, t ← the distance from p to c and the t of its nearest point
      o ← |dot(direction at t, unit(p − point_at(c, t)))| if t is 0 or 1, else 0
      if best is none, or d < best.d − 10⁻¹², or (|d − best.d| ≤ 10⁻¹² and o < best.o):
        best ← (c, t, d, o)
    value[ch] ← clamp(pseudo_distance(p, best.c, best.t, best.d), −spread, spread)
  answer value

pseudo_distance(p, c, t, d):
  if (t = 0 and dot(p − c's start, start direction) < 0)
     or (t = 1 and dot(p − c's end, end direction) > 0):
    side ← cross(that direction, p − that end)          // past the end: the tangent line
    answer −|side| if side > 0 else |side|
  side ← cross(direction at t, p − point_at(c, t))
  answer −d if side > 0 else d                   // inside is on the right

The plate is one field, an ampersand's, used four ways, each a line over the same array: its bands; a threshold, which is the fill; an absolute value, which is the outline; and a falloff, which is the glow. The title is the payoff for the atlas: eight glyphs baked at 32 pixels into four channels each, drawn five times bigger, with a shadow and a glow read from the true channel and the fill and the outline from the median, every one of them a formula on a distance.
