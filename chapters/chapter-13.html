Chapter Thirteen

Stroking is Filling

The renderer can fill any shape you can describe, paint it with anything, clip it, group it. It cannot draw a line. Not a real line — one with width, the thing every drawing program calls a stroke. And here's the surprise that runs the next three chapters: a stroke is not a new kind of drawing. It's a shape. You take the path, push each segment out by half the width on both sides, cap the ends, fill the corners, and you've built an outline — an ordinary closed path — that you hand to the fill from chapter 7. The renderer still has exactly one drawing operation. Everything else is a path factory.

That reframing is the whole part. Stroking, offsetting curves, dashing — three chapters, and not one line of new rasterizer code between them. This chapter does the straight-line case: a path made of segments, stroked into an outline. It sounds easy and it is mostly easy, but the corners and the ends hide more geometric fiddliness than anything in Part II, and the degenerate inputs — zero-length segments, doubled points, hairpin reversals — are all in real files and all divide by zero if you're careless.

§ 13.1 The pieces

Start with one segment. Stroking it is chapter 3's reveal made real: a line of width w is the rectangle of half-width h = w/2 centred on the segment. Take the segment's direction, take its perpendicular, and the four corners are the ends offset by ±h along that perpendicular. A whole path is a rectangle per segment. But the rectangles don't meet cleanly: at every interior vertex there's a wedge of gap on the outside of the turn, and at every open end the rectangle stops flat. Those are the two remaining pieces — joins and caps — and each comes in three flavours.

A join fills the outer gap between two segments. Bevel is the simplest — a triangle straight across the gap. Round is an arc of radius h about the vertex, from one outer offset point to the other, and it has to go the short way round: the long way is the inside of the turn, and an arc swept that way leaves a notch at the corner where the gap should have been filled. (The first draft of this chapter did exactly that, and three readers reproduced the notch faithfully.) Miter extends the two outer edges until they meet at a point, the sharp corner you'd draw by hand. A cap closes an open end: butt stops flat at the endpoint, square extends a half-width past it, round is a semicircle. Six little shapes, and every stroke in every vector program is made of them.

§ 13.2 The stroker

stroke_to_path assembles the pieces. For each subpath it emits a rectangle per segment, a join wedge per interior vertex, and a cap per open end, all as subpaths of one path. Fill that path nonzero and the pieces union into the stroke — the overlaps on the inside of each turn are wound twice, still inside, and vanish into the fill. There's no boundary-tracing, no boolean union, no special case for self-overlap; nonzero winding does the merging for free. The scenarios pin a single segment to the rectangle it must become, then check that each join style produces its own shape, and that the miter reaches its tip where the geometry says it should.

Free, on one condition: every piece has to wind the same way. Nonzero counts signed crossings. A rectangle wound one way overlapping a wedge wound the other way sums to zero, and zero is a hole, sitting exactly where two pieces overlap. So the stroker orients every piece before it emits it: counterclockwise on screen, which is to say chapter 7's polygon_area comes out negative, and any piece that comes out positive has its points reversed. This is the kind of bug that hides. On a single gentle corner the only overlaps are between neighbours, and neighbours happen to agree, so the chevron looks fine either way. Stroke a wide line around a tight bend and rectangles from across the bend reach over the joins; if the joins wind the wrong way, the fill is full of holes. That's what the u_turn() scenario does: nine points on the upper half of a circle of radius 10 about (50, 50), from 180° to 360° in steps of 22.5°, stroked 40 wide, so the stroke overlaps itself all the way round. Every pixel near the bend must come out fully covered.

stroke_to_path(path, width, cap, join, miter_limit):
  h ← width / 2;  out ← path()
  for subpath in path:
    pts ← drop duplicate consecutive points; if closed and pts ends where it began, drop that end too
    if pts has one point:  emit a dot for round caps, a square for square, else nothing
    for each segment (a, b):  emit the rectangle of half-width h on a..b
    for each interior vertex v:  emit join(v, dir_in, dir_out, h, join, miter_limit)
    if subpath is open:  emit cap at each end
  return out                       // fill it nonzero; that is the stroke

emit(piece):
  if polygon_area(piece) > 0:  reverse its points   // every piece winds the same way
  append piece to out

A closed subpath is the same, with two differences: it has a segment from the last point back to the first, so it gets a join at every vertex including that closing one, and it has no ends, so no caps. A stroked closed triangle is three rectangles and three joins, and it fills into a ring.

§ 13.3 The miter, and knowing when to give up on it

The miter is the one join with a failure mode. Its tip sits at distance miter_length = h / sin(θ/2) from the vertex, where θ is the interior angle of the corner — the angle you'd measure inside the bend, between the incoming edge and the outgoing one, which is the angle from the reversed incoming direction to the outgoing direction. A straight-through path has θ = 180° and no miter to speak of; a sharp corner has a small θ. As the angle gets sharp, sin(θ/2) goes to zero and the tip shoots off toward infinity — a nearly-doubled-back path would grow a miter spike a mile long. So there's a miter_limit: cap the ratio miter_length / h, and when a join exceeds it, fall back to a bevel. The default limit is 4, which switches to bevel at about 29°. The scenarios check the length against the closed form, and check that the same chevron miters at one limit and bevels at a tighter one, switching exactly at the ratio.

Feature: The miter, and its limit

  the half-width over the sine of half the turn. On a sharp angle that distance
  runs away to infinity, so a miter_limit caps the ratio miter_length / h, and

§ 13.4 The inputs that break a naive stroker

The trap

Every step above took a segment's direction, which means dividing by its length. Real paths hand you segments of length zero: a subpath of a single point, two identical points in a row from a sloppy export, a path that doubles exactly back on itself. Each one divides by zero in the normal calculation, and all three are in files you'll be asked to render. The fixes are small and mandatory. Drop duplicate consecutive points before you start, so a doubled point never becomes a zero-length segment. A closed subpath whose last point is its first (every glyph contour in chapter 16 arrives this way) would get a zero-length closing segment, so drop that last point too. A subpath of one point is not an error to reject — it's a dot, and with a round cap it's a filled disc of radius h, a construct the SVG spec calls out by name for drawing a single round point. With a butt cap the same dot is nothing at all, correctly. The near-hairpin reversal, where the direction almost flips 180°, has no well-defined miter — the tip runs off toward infinity, and the miter limit catches it and bevels. At an exact 180° reversal the two offset edges are collinear, the outer gap closes to nothing, and there's no join to emit at all — the segments' rectangles already overlap. None of these is deep; all of them are the difference between a stroker that works on your test path and one that works on the web.

§ 13.5 Putting it together

The plate is a chevron stroked three ways, and it shows the trick in the open: the gray is the fill, and the magenta drawn over it is the outline the stroker generated. Miter keeps the point, round softens it, bevel cuts it flat — and in every panel the gray is nothing but a nonzero fill of the magenta. The little crossing you can see on the inside of each turn is the two segment rectangles overlapping; nonzero winding fills straight through it.

joins_plate():
  for join in "miter", "round", "bevel":
    outline ← stroke_to_path(chevron(), 26, "butt", join, 4)
    fill outline nonzero in gray
    draw each edge of outline in magenta          // line_wu, endpoints rounded to nearest, halves up
  return the three panels side by side

plate_13():
  return magnify(joins_plate(), 2)

A stroke turned out to be a fill of a path you build. The same will be true of the next two chapters and it never stops being true: offsetting a curve, in chapter 14, is building that same outline when the segments are Béziers instead of lines, which is harder than it sounds because the offset of a curve isn't a curve. And dashing, in chapter 15, is a path-to-path transform that runs before the stroker even sees the path. The fill from chapter 7 does all the drawing to the very end of the book.
