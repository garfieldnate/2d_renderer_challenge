Chapter Ten

Paint Servers and Gradients

A shape has always been one flat colour. That's about to look very limited. The move this chapter makes is small to say and large in what it unlocks: stop thinking of paint as a colour and start thinking of it as a function — give it a point on the canvas, it hands back a colour. A solid fill is the constant function. A gradient is a function that turns the point into a number and looks it up. And once paint is a function of position, gradients, patterns and, in the next chapter, whole images stop being three features and become one.

Here's the reframing, concretely. Chapter 2's paint_through took a coverage buffer and a single colour and blended that one colour everywhere the coverage was nonzero. Replace the colour with a Paint — anything answering paint_at(paint, x, y) — and sample it afresh at every pixel. Nothing else changes. A solid paint ignores the point and returns its colour, so it behaves exactly as before. A gradient paint does the interesting part: it projects the point down to a single number between 0 and 1, and reads a colour off a table. Everything in this chapter is a different way of computing that one number.

§ 10.1 The stop table

The table first, since all three gradients share it. A gradient is a list of stops, each an offset in [0, 1] and a colour, sorted by offset. sample_stops(stops, t) is the colour at parameter t: below the first stop you get the first colour, above the last you get the last, and between two stops a straight blend, in linear light, weighted by where t falls between their offsets. With more than a couple of stops a binary search finds the bracketing pair. Interpolating in linear light is the book's convention from chapter 1; browsers blend gradient stops in the encoded space instead, so a midpoint that reads as an even grey here looks lighter there. Same stops, different lane, and the book has been in this lane the whole time.

§ 10.2 Extend modes

The parameter a gradient computes won't always land in [0, 1] — a point can sit before the start of a linear axis or well past its end. extend(t, mode) decides what happens out there. Pad clamps to the ends, so the gradient holds its first and last colours forever. Repeat wraps, tiling the ramp over and over. Reflect bounces, mirroring the ramp every unit so it meets itself without a seam. Three lines, and they're the difference between a gradient that fills a shape and one that tiles a background.

§ 10.3 The three gradients

Now the three ways to turn a point into a parameter. A linear gradient has an axis, two points p0 and p1. The parameter of a point is how far along that axis it projects, as a fraction of the axis length — a dot product over the squared length. On the axis, the parameter is exactly the fraction of the way from p0 to p1, and perpendicular to the axis it doesn't change, which is why a linear gradient comes out in stripes square to its axis. That's the whole of linear_t.

A radial gradient is where it gets fun. The general form is two circles — a start circle at t = 0 and an end circle at t = 1 — and the parameter of a point is the t of the interpolated circle that passes through it. Interpolate the centres and the radii, write "the point is on that circle", and you get a quadratic in t; solve it and take the larger root whose radius isn't negative. When the two circles are concentric it's the plain radial everyone knows, parameter equal to distance over radius. Set the start radius to zero and offset its centre and you get SVG's focal gradient, the highlight that sits off to one side of a sphere.

radial_t(point):                       // t of the interpolated circle through the point
  cd ← c1 − c0;  dr ← r1 − r0;  pd ← point − c0
  a ← cd·cd − dr²
  b ← −2 · (pd·cd + r0·dr)
  c ← pd·pd − r0²
  solve a·t² + b·t + c = 0
  return the largest root t with r0 + t·dr ≥ 0, or none if there is none

A conic gradient, last, sweeps the parameter around a centre: the angle from the centre to the point, divided by a full turn, wrapping once around. It's the one gradient that's periodic by nature, and it's how you draw a colour wheel or a pie chart in one paint.

The trap

The focal gradient hides a nasty case. When the focal point — the start circle, radius zero — sits outside the end circle, there's a whole region of the plane that no circle in the family reaches. Draw a ray from the focal point through such a pixel and it never crosses the end circle, so the quadratic has no root with a valid radius, and radial_t returns none. A naive implementation takes that none, or the NaN it got instead, and paints black: a hard black wedge fanning out from the focal point across half the shape. The spec's fix is to say those unreachable pixels take the last stop's colour, as if the gradient ran out — which is what paint_at does with a none. It's not beautiful, but it's a colour from the gradient rather than a black hole, and the scenario pins a point in the cone to prove it. If your focal gradient grows a dark spike, this is the line.

§ 10.4 Painting with a paint

paint_fill(c, cov, paint) is paint_through with the colour replaced by a function: for every covered pixel it samples paint_at at the pixel's centre and blends that colour in through the coverage, in linear light. Hand it a solid paint and it is paint_through exactly, byte for byte — which the scenario checks, because a generalization that changed the old behaviour would be a regression, not a generalization. Hand it a gradient and every pixel gets its own colour. That single substitution is the entire chapter; everything before it was working out what paint_at should return.

Sampling once per pixel

The paint is sampled at the pixel centre, one colour for the whole pixel, so a gradient that changes fast across a pixel is point-sampled and can alias the way a hard edge did in chapter 2. In practice gradients change slowly and it never shows; when it does, the fix is the same supersampling chapter 2 used, or the analytic coverage from chapter 7 with the paint sampled at the covered area's centroid. The book samples once and moves on.

§ 10.5 Eight bits isn't enough

Paint a slow, dark gradient and look closely: it comes out in stripes. Not because the maths is stepped — the light values are a smooth ramp — but because the output is eight bits per channel, and a stretch of the ramp that spans less than one code value all rounds to the same byte. The eye is very good at finding those edges. The cure is ordered dithering: before rounding each pixel down to a byte, nudge it by a threshold that depends on its position, taken from a small repeating matrix. A pixel whose true value sits 0.3 of the way between two bytes rounds up in 30% of the positions in the tile and down in the rest, so a flat band becomes a fine stipple of the two neighbouring bytes and the eye blends it back to the value in between. The matrix is the 4×4 Bayer matrix, and the whole trick is one addition before the floor.

In the GUI

The gradient tool's stop editor — the little bar with colour stops you drag — is this chapter's stop table, and the Linear / Radial / Angle menu beside it is linear_t, radial_t and conic_t. Illustrator's gradient annotator, the bar drawn over the shape, is the axis p0 to p1.

§ 10.6 Putting it together

The plate is one stop table — a sunset, four colours dark to pale — addressed three ways: a linear ramp across the diagonal, a focal radial with its highlight up and to the left, and a conic sweep. Nothing about the stops changed between the three panels. Only the function from point to parameter did. That's the chapter in one picture: paint is a function, and a gradient is that function wearing three different hats.

SUNSET ← [stop(0.0,  color(0.05, 0.02, 0.15)),      // deep violet
          stop(0.35, color(0.75, 0.15, 0.25)),      // red
          stop(0.7,  color(0.98, 0.6,  0.15)),      // orange
          stop(1.0,  color(1.0,  0.95, 0.75))]      // pale

three_gradients():
  lin ← linear_gradient(point(10, 10), point(140, 140), SUNSET, "pad")
  rad ← radial_gradient(point(55, 55), 0, point(75, 75), 85, SUNSET, "pad")   // focal
  con ← conic_gradient(point(75, 75), −π/2, SUNSET, "pad")
  for paint in lin, rad, con:
    panel ← a 150×150 canvas;  paint_fill(panel, full coverage, paint)
    place panel left to right, 4 px of paper between them
  return the row

plate_10():
  return three_gradients()

extend_strip():                          // one short gradient under the three modes, ×2
  stops ← [stop(0, color(0.1, 0.15, 0.5)), stop(1, color(1.0, 0.7, 0.1))]
  c ← canvas(180, 90)
  for mode, i in "pad", "repeat", "reflect":
    g ← linear_gradient(point(60, 0), point(100, 0), stops, mode)   // axis is the middle third
    fill the band of rows [30i, 30i + 30) with g
  return magnify(c, 2)
