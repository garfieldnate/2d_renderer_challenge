Chapter Seventeen

Rasterizing Type Well

Set the word Hamburg at eleven pixels, black on white, with the fill from chapter 7 and the blending from chapter 1. It's legible. It's also thin and grey, and next to the same word in any application on your screen it looks like a photocopy of itself. Nothing is wrong. Chapter 7's coverage is exact and chapter 1's blending is physically correct, and the result is that a one-pixel stem that lands across two pixel columns is two half-covered columns, each mixed in linear light to a grey that your eye reads as much lighter than half. Every text stack on earth has looked at this and cheated, and this chapter is about the cheats: where the glyph sits within a pixel, how to stop rasterizing it over and over, the fudge factor everyone applies and nobody names, and the trick of measuring coverage three times per pixel and steering it into the coloured stripes the pixel is made of.

None of this changes the renderer. Every picture in the chapter is a chapter 7 fill of a chapter 16 path, composited by chapter 1's mix. What changes is what goes in and how the result is kept.

§ 17.1 A quarter of a pixel

Text is set with a pen that moves by fractional amounts: Roboto's H at eleven pixels advances the pen by 7.8418. If you round the pen to a whole pixel before drawing each glyph, every letter lands up to half a pixel from where it should, and the spacing of a word wobbles by a pixel here and there in a way you'll see before you can say what's wrong. If you don't round, every glyph is rasterized fresh at its own fractional position, which is correct and slow. The compromise is the one every renderer makes: quarter pixels. subpixel_of(x) splits a pen position into a whole pixel and one of four quarters, the fraction rounded to the nearest quarter, carrying into the next pixel when it rounds all the way up. Four positions is few enough to cache and fine enough that the wobble disappears.

A glyph rendered at a quarter is a bitmap: its coverage in a buffer of its own, no bigger than it needs, plus two integers saying where the buffer's top-left corner sits relative to the pen. glyph_bitmap(font, name, size, subpixel) takes the glyph's bounds from chapter 16, scales them, shifts them right by the quarter, and rounds outward to whole pixels: the buffer's columns run from floor(xmin) to ceil(xmax) − 1, its rows likewise with y turned over, and the path is filled into it through a text_matrix whose origin is the quarter, offset by the buffer's corner. The same glyph at a different quarter is a different bitmap, and the scenario pins the thing that makes the whole idea honest: the ink is identical. Chapter 7's coverage is the area, and moving a shape a quarter of a pixel doesn't change its area. paint_bitmap puts a bitmap on the canvas with its pen at a whole pixel, each covered pixel mixed toward the colour by its coverage.

§ 17.2 The cache and the atlas

A page of text is a few thousand glyphs drawn from a few dozen shapes at one size. Rasterizing each one is the expensive part and the pointless part, since the e at quarter two is the same bitmap every time it appears. So keep them: glyph_cache is a table keyed by (glyph, size, subpixel), and cached_bitmap renders on the first request and hands back the very same bitmap on every request after. Four quarters times the sizes in use times the glyphs in use is the whole cost of a page, paid once.

Where the bitmaps live is the other half. A renderer that composites on a GPU can't upload a thousand tiny textures, so it packs them into one big one, an atlas, and remembers where each went. The packing rule this chapter uses is the simplest that works: shelves. Bitmaps go left to right along a shelf whose height is set by its first bitmap; when the next one doesn't fit on the shelf, a new shelf opens below the tallest so far. It wastes a little space at the end of each shelf and a little above the short bitmaps, and it never has to move anything. atlas_add copies a bitmap in and answers where, or none when there is no room. The scenario packs four rectangles by hand so you can draw the shelves on paper, then two real glyphs.

atlas_add(atlas, bitmap):
  if bitmap.width > atlas.width or bitmap.height > atlas.height:  return none
  if shelf_x + bitmap.width > atlas.width:            // no room on this shelf: open one below
    shelf_y ← shelf_y + shelf_h;  shelf_x ← 0;  shelf_h ← 0
  if shelf_y + max(bitmap.height, shelf_h) > atlas.height:  return none
  copy the bitmap's coverage into the atlas at (shelf_x, shelf_y)
  corner ← (shelf_x, shelf_y);  shelf_x ← shelf_x + bitmap.width;  shelf_h ← max(shelf_h, bitmap.height)
  return corner                 // shelf_x, shelf_y and shelf_h start at 0

§ 17.3 The fudge, named

Now the thin grey text. Take one column of the l in Figure 17.1 at coverage 0.16: chapter 1 mixes black into white by 16% in linear light, which encodes to a byte of 236, a grey so pale it's nearly not there. What you're used to seeing is the other lane from chapter 1: blend the file values instead of the light, and the same pixel comes out at 214. Physically that's wrong. Perceptually it's what every operating system and browser does for text, because text is small, high-contrast and read at a glance, and the correct blend makes it look starved. The book's switch is still there: paint_bitmap takes a linear flag, and the scenario pins both values so you can see exactly what the cheat buys.

The second cheat is subtler and every bit as universal. Even blended the wrong way, a font designed for print looks lighter on screen than on paper, because paper ink spreads and pixels don't. So renderers make the stems a little fatter: stem darkening, which is emboldening by a fraction of a pixel. You have the tool: embolden(font, name, size, amount) is the glyph's fill plus chapter 13's stroke of its outline, amount wide, the two coverages added and clamped to one, in a bitmap grown a pixel all round to make room. A third of a pixel is plenty. Figure 17.3 sets the word three ways. The middle row is what you've been reading all your life; the top row is the truth; the bottom row is the truth with heavier stems, and it's the one a renderer that refuses to blend in encoded space would ship.

In the GUI

macOS's Use font smoothing when available was a stem-darkening switch, and Windows' ClearType Text Tuner lets you pick the gamma of the blend. Photoshop's Character panel has an anti-aliasing menu, None, Sharp, Crisp, Strong, Smooth: five fudge factors with names. Hinting, the grid-fitting of outlines, isn't here; it is dying with low-resolution screens.

§ 17.4 Three coverages per pixel

A pixel on an LCD isn't a dot. It's three vertical stripes, red, green and blue, side by side, and your eye fuses them. Which means a display has three times the horizontal resolution it admits to, if you're willing to colour the edges. Rasterize the glyph three times wider, so each stripe gets its own coverage, then mix red through the first stripe's coverage, green through the second, blue through the third. A stem edge that lands on the boundary between stripes stops being a grey column and becomes a red-tinged column on one side and a blue-tinged one on the other, and at reading size the tinge vanishes into a sharper stem. That's the whole idea, and on its own it looks terrible: the colour fringes are strong enough to see. The fix is a filter along the row: replace every stripe's coverage with the average of itself and its two neighbours. Three taps that sum to one, so the ink is spread across neighbouring stripes but never lost; the fringes soften and the stem stays sharper than grey. lcd_filter is the filter, lcd_coverage is the wide rasterization with every row filtered, and paint_lcd mixes each channel through its own stripe. The scenarios check that the filter spreads a spike into three, that the wide buffer holds exactly three times the grey buffer's ink, and that each channel of a pixel took its own stripe.

The trap

All of it assumes you know the physical order of the stripes, that they're vertical, and that your pixels reach the glass unchanged. Turn the display through ninety degrees and the stripes are horizontal and the filter fringes every stem. Plenty of OLED panels arrange their subpixels in a diamond, or share them between pixels, and there is no row to filter along. Composite the text through a transform, scale it, fade it, put it in a layer that the compositor resamples, and the carefully steered stripes land on the wrong hardware and become plain colour fringes. That's why the industry has been quietly abandoning it: macOS dropped LCD text rendering in 2018, and high-resolution screens took away most of the reason for it. It's in the book because it's clever, because you'll meet it in every renderer older than that, and because the row filter is a two-line lesson in what a filter's taps mean.

Every trick in this chapter is a decision about what to feed the fill and what to do with what comes out. Chapter 18 puts the glyphs in a row: advances, kerning, a baseline, and the moment you discover a text run is a list of positions handed to chapter 16.
