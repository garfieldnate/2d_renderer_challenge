Chapter Eight

Curves

Chapter 5's circle_path was a fib. It's a twelve-sided polygon wearing a circle's name, and up close it has corners. Every smooth thing you'll ever want to draw — a letter, a logo, the tiger's back — is curved, and so far the renderer can't say the word. This chapter adds the one curve that graphics actually uses, the Bézier, and then does the only thing the rasterizer will accept: turns it back into straight lines, but adaptively, so the corners land where the curve actually bends and nowhere else. The fill from chapter 7 never learns what a curve is. It doesn't have to.

Here's the arrangement the whole chapter turns on, so it isn't a surprise later. The rasterizer you spent two chapters on eats polygons. It's fast and it's exact and you're not going to replace it. So a curve's job is not to be drawn — it's to be flattened, chopped into a polyline close enough to the real thing that no one can see the difference, and handed to the fill as an ordinary path. That's why chapter 7 could get away with only knowing about line segments: by the time anything reaches it, every curve in the book is already a run of tiny straight lines. The art is in chopping well — few segments where the curve is nearly straight, many where it turns hard — and in doing the chopping at the right moment, which turns out to be after the shape has been scaled to its final size, not before.

§ 8.1 A curve is its control points

A Bézier curve is defined by a handful of points and a rule for blending them. A quadratic has three control points; a cubic has four. The curve starts at the first point and ends at the last, and the ones in between pull it in their direction without ever being touched — the curve leans toward them but passes through only its two ends. Those interior points are exactly the handles you drag in a pen tool. Three points and four points are the only two cases the book needs, and everything below works for both.

To find a point on the curve, blend the control points. The clean way to do it is de Casteljau's construction: interpolate between each neighbouring pair of control points by the fraction t, which gives one fewer point; do it again on those; keep going until one point is left, and that's the point on the curve. point_at(c, t) is that construction. It's a few lines, it never needs a power of t, and it's numerically kinder than expanding the polynomial. A quadratic reaches its middle at point_at(q, 0.5); a symmetric arch peaks lower than its handles, which the tight bounds in §8.2 will make precise.

That same construction, kept rather than thrown away, splits a curve in two. split_at(c, t) hands back two curves of the same degree: the piece from 0 to t and the piece from t to 1, whose control points are the left and right edges of the de Casteljau pyramid. Split a curve and evaluate the halves and you retrace the original exactly — the left half at parameter 1 is the original at t, the right half at 0 is the same point, and everything in between lines up after rescaling. That's not a coincidence to test around; it's the property the whole flattening algorithm leans on, so pin it. And derivative(c, t) gives the tangent vector, which points backward when the curve is heading back the way it came — a fact strokes will need in chapter 13, tested here so it's not a surprise then.

§ 8.2 Tight bounds

Filling wants a bounding box, so it can skip the rows and columns a shape doesn't touch. The lazy box is the one around the control points, and it's correct but loose: since the curve never reaches its interior handles, that box has slack on every side that a handle sticks out. The arch cubic((0,0), (0,4), (4,4), (4,0)) has handles at height 4 but the curve tops out at 3, so a quarter of the lazy box is empty. Usually loose is fine. When the box drives how finely you subdivide, or feeds a spatial index later, loose costs real time, so the book computes the tight one.

curve_bounds(c) is exact. A curve turns around in x or y only where that component of its derivative is zero, so solve the derivative for its roots — a quadratic's derivative is linear and has at most one root per axis, a cubic's is quadratic and has at most two — keep the roots between 0 and 1, evaluate the curve there and at both ends, and take the smallest and largest. That's the tight box, and the root finding is the same small algebra a dozen later things will want. The scenarios pin a cubic that curls up well inside its handles, the arch that peaks at 3, and a quadratic whose single turning point sets its height.

features/chapter08-bounds.feature

Feature: Tight bounds
  curve_bounds(c) is the smallest axis-aligned box that holds the curve

  points, so the curve turns back before it reaches them. The tight box is
  found by evaluating the curve at its ends and at every parameter where it
  turns around in x or y, which is where a component of the derivative is zero.

    Given q ← quadratic(point(0, 0), point(2, 4), point(4, 0))
    Then  curve_bounds(q) = (0, 0, 4, 2)

§ 8.3 Flattening

Now the step that lets the fill do its job: turn the curve into a polyline. The measure of how badly a straight line would fake a curve is flatness(c) — the greatest distance of an interior control point from the straight chord between the curve's ends. A curve whose handles sit right on that chord is already a line and has flatness 0. The bigger the flatness, the worse a single segment would be, and the fix is to cut the curve in half with split_at and ask each half. Halves of a gentle curve are flat almost at once; halves near a tight bend stay curved and get cut again. Recurse until every piece is flatter than the tolerance, and collect the endpoints.

flatten(c, tolerance) does exactly that and returns the list of points, first to last, both ends always included. A straight cubic comes back as its two endpoints and nothing else. A hard-bending one comes back with points bunched at the bend and sparse on the straights, which is the whole point of doing it adaptively rather than chopping into a fixed number of equal steps. As the tolerance shrinks the polyline gets longer and its length climbs toward the curve's true arc length — a satisfying thing to watch, and the test that proves flattening converges rather than merely terminating.

One thing about when to flatten, because it's the mistake everyone makes once. The tolerance is a distance in pixels — a quarter of a pixel is plenty — and pixels only exist after the shape has been scaled to its final size on the canvas. Flatten a curve at unit size, then scale the polyline up by fifty, and you scale the error up by fifty too: the facets that were invisible become a coarse dodecagon. So flatten after the transform, not before. Because a Bézier is affine, transform_curve then flatten gives the same curve as flatten then transform — but the tolerance is now measured in device pixels, where it belongs, and the big shape gets the extra segments it needs. That's why the flowers at the end are all equally smooth no matter their size, and it's the reason vector-effect exists in SVG.

That teardrop is two cubics — a round top and a sharp bottom — flattened at tolerance 4 on the left and 0.1 on the right, then filled:

teardrop():                           // two cubics in a 60×60 box, a round top and a pointed bottom
  right ← cubic(point(30.5, 12), point(58, 16), point(46, 52), point(30.5, 52))
  left  ← cubic(point(30.5, 52), point(15, 52), point(3, 16), point(30.5, 12))
  return right, left

drops():                              // the teardrop coarse (tol 4) on the left, fine (tol 0.1) on the right, ×4
  for (tol, panel) in (4.0, left), (0.1, right):
    q ← path();  flatten_into_path(q, right_cubic, tol);  flatten_into_path(q, left_cubic, tol);  close(q)
    paint_through(panel, fill_path(q, "nonzero", 60, 60), INKS[2])
  return magnify(side_by_side(left, right), 4)

§ 8.4 The elliptical arc

Béziers are most of what you need, but SVG paths also carry an elliptical arc, the A command, and it's specified in a way that is genuinely miserable to unpick. You give it the point to end at, two radii, a rotation for the ellipse, and two one-bit flags — and from that it has to recover which ellipse you meant and which piece of it to travel. There are four arcs that fit any two points and a pair of radii: two ellipses, mirror images, and on each the short way round or the long way. The flags large_arc and sweep pick one of the four. None of this is hard mathematics, but every step has a sign that's easy to get backward, and the spec hides them in an appendix. The book works it in full so you don't have to.

arc(...) takes SVG's endpoint form and returns the center form you can actually walk: a center, the radii, the rotation, a start angle and a swept angle. arc_point(a, t) then walks it from t = 0 at the start to t = 1 at the end. The test that nails the four-way choice is to fix the endpoints and radii and flip the two flags through all four combinations: same two points, four different arcs, four different midpoints. With a circle of radius 5 between (0, 0) and (6, 0), the four midpoints land at (3, ±1) for the short arcs and (3, ±9) for the long ones, and every one of the four still passes through both endpoints.

The trap

Two more things in that conversion will bite, and both have a scenario because otherwise you'll blame the wrong code. First, the radii can be too small: if you ask for a radius-5 arc between points 12 apart, no such circle exists, and the spec's rule is to grow both radii by the same factor until the ellipse barely reaches — not to error, not to clamp one radius, but to scale them together. Skip it and you take the square root of a negative number and get a NaN arc that paints nothing or everything. It has a twin a step later: the angle between the two spokes is an acos, and for a half circle the value handed to acos lands exactly on the edge of its domain, so a hair of rounding pushes it past 1 and acos returns a NaN or throws. Clamp the argument to [-1, 1] before the acos; the half-circle scenario is there to make you. Second, the rotation is in radians here to match the rest of the book, but SVG's A command writes it in degrees — convert at the parser in chapter 20, not here, and write a comment where you do, because a stray factor of π/180 in an arc is invisible until the ellipse is subtly the wrong shape. The arc is the one place in the book where the spec is the adversary; the scenarios are your proof you've read it correctly.

In the GUI

The pen tool's two handles on an anchor are the interior control points of the curves meeting there, and the smooth-versus-corner toggle is whether the incoming and outgoing handles are forced collinear. Illustrator's Object → Path → Flatten is this section's function with the tolerance on a slider — run it and the curves become the polyline you'd have handed the fill.

§ 8.5 Putting it together

Something that was a lie one chapter ago. A flower, its petals actual curves. One petal is two cubics, tip to base and back, bulging out to the sides; a ring of them around a center is a flower; and three flowers at three sizes go on the page. The trick from §8.3 is the whole show: each petal is transformed to its place and size first, then flattened in that device space at one shared tolerance, so the big flower gets more segments than the small one and all three come out equally smooth. The center of each is punched out by filling a circle in the paper colour, the same take-away move the sunburst used.

petal():                              // one petal, tip at (0,−1) and base at the origin, from two cubics
  right ← cubic(point(0, 0), point(0.55, −0.35), point(0.4, −0.92), point(0, −1))
  left  ← cubic(point(0, −1), point(−0.4, −0.92), point(−0.55, −0.35), point(0, 0))
  return right, left

flower_at(p, m, n, tolerance):        // add n petals around the origin, placed by m
  right, left ← petal()
  for k in 0..n-1:
    spin ← m * rotation(2π · k / n)
    flatten_into_path(p, transform_curve(right, spin), tolerance)   // flatten in device space
    flatten_into_path(p, transform_curve(left,  spin), tolerance)
    close(p)

flower():
  c ← canvas(360, 360);  fill(c, PAPER)
  spots ← [(108, 250, 44, 8, 0.0), (200, 145, 74, 8, 0.39), (286, 252, 54, 7, 0.8)]   // cx, cy, scale, petals, turn
  for (cx, cy, s, n, rot), i in spots:
    m ← translation(cx, cy) * scaling(s, s) * rotation(rot)
    petals ← path();  flower_at(petals, m, n, 0.2)
    paint_through(c, fill_path(petals, "nonzero", 360, 360), INKS[i mod 3])
    disc ← circle_path(cx, cy, s · 0.3, 64)
    paint_through(c, fill_path(disc, "nonzero", 360, 360), PAPER)   // punch the middle
  return c

plate_08():
  return magnify(flower(), 2)

The path can hold curves, the curves know how to become lines, and the lines were always going to be filled exactly. That closes the core engine: from here a shape is whatever you can describe with moves, lines, curves and arcs, and the renderer will fill it, antialiased, at any size. What it can't yet do is care what colour goes through the coverage, or what happens where two filled shapes overlap — the seam chapter 7 warned about. That's chapter 9, and it's where the book stops being about coverage and starts being about paint.
