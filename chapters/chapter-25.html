Chapter Twenty-Five

The Raster Editor Detour

Everything so far compiled geometry into pixels: somebody wrote down a path, and the renderer worked out which pixels it covered. A paint program runs the other way. There's no path, only pixels and a hand on a mouse, and the mouse reports where it was sixty times a second whether the hand moved a millimetre or a foot. Drag a brush fast in a program that trusts those reports and you get a string of beads. Pour a bucket into a circle you drew with antialiasing and you get a grey ring around the paint, the halo, that no tolerance setting quite removes. Those two bugs have shipped in more paint programs than anyone would admit, and this chapter builds both, then fixes them, with the coverage machinery from chapter 2.

This is a detour, and it can be read any time after chapter 3. It has four parts, each short: brushes, the bucket, indexed colour, and the pair of features that make an editor an editor, selection and undo. The idea under all four is the book's usual one. A raster editor's tools are the same coverage machinery with the geometry supplied by a hand instead of a path.

§ 25.1 Brushes

A brush is a coverage kernel, a dab, stamped along the pointer's path. The dab here is round, fully covered inside hardness × radius and falling in a straight ramp to nothing at the radius, so a hardness of 1 is a hard disc and a hardness of 0 is a soft cone. Stamping a dab doesn't paint it straight onto the canvas; it adds it to the stroke's own mask, 1 − (1 − m)(1 − flow × k), the same union chapter 12 used for clips, so overlapping dabs build up toward full coverage. Flow is how much each dab adds. The finished mask is scaled by the stroke's opacity and painted through once, which is why a stroke at 50% opacity never gets darker than 50% however many times it crosses itself.

Where do the dabs go? The obvious answer is at every event the pointer sends, and it's wrong. Events come at a steady rate in time, and a hand moves at a very unsteady rate in space: slow at the start of a stroke and quick in the middle. wobbly_events() is 25 events bunched where the hand was slow. One dab per event paints a clot at the start and a row of beads across the rest, with bare paper between them. Instead, walk along the polyline through the events and drop a dab every spacing × diameter of distance, carrying what's left of the step from one segment into the next the way chapter 15 carried dashes. Now the dabs are evenly spaced wherever the hand went. The same 25 events make 99 dabs, and the weakest point along the stroke is 70% covered where the one-per-event stroke had bare paper.

§ 25.2 Flood fill

The bucket fills the region around a pixel: every pixel you can reach from it, stepping to a neighbour, without leaving the pixels that match it. "Match" means every channel's byte within the tolerance of the seed's. "Neighbour" means the four pixels that share an edge, or the eight that share an edge or a corner; with eight, the fill leaks through any diagonal gap in a one-pixel line, which is why four is the default.

The trap

The flood fill everyone writes first is four lines long and calls itself on its four neighbours. It works on small regions and then blows the stack on a big one, because the recursion goes as deep as the region is large: filling an empty 200 by 200 canvas from a corner puts 40,000 calls in flight at once, and Python stops at a thousand, a C program at whatever its stack allows. The scanline fill does the same job with a stack of seeds: take one, run left and right along its row while the pixels match, mark the whole run, and push one seed for every matching run in the rows above and below. On that empty canvas it pushes 200 seeds and never holds more than one at a time. It's faster too, because a run is marked in one sweep. It's the only version worth teaching.

Now pour a bucket into the ring. The ring was drawn with chapter 13's stroke and chapter 7's exact coverage, so its edge isn't a line between paper and ink; it's a pixel or two of every grey in between. At tolerance 0 the fill stops at the first pixel that isn't pure paper, and those greys are left standing between the paint and the ring: the halo. Raise the tolerance and the fill eats into the greys, but it can only take them whole or leave them, and the paint's edge is now as jagged as its tolerance. By 254 it's leaked right out of the ring. The Anti-alias box is the other half of the fight: it gives every pixel bordering the fill from outside half the paint, which softens the edge and fills the halo halfway. Every bucket in every editor has a tolerance slider and an anti-alias checkbox, and they fight each other, because neither knows the one thing that would solve the problem, which is how much of each grey pixel was ink. The picture no longer says. A path fill knows exactly, which is the case for keeping the path.

§ 25.3 Quantization

An indexed-colour image stores a palette of at most 256 colours and one byte per pixel naming one of them. Making one is two problems: choosing the palette, and deciding what to do with every pixel that isn't in it.

Heckbert's median cut chooses the palette. Put every colour the image uses in one box. Find the box that's widest in some channel, sort it along that channel, and cut it where half its pixels are on each side, so the palette spends its entries where the pixels are. Repeat until there are as many boxes as you want colours, and each box's average is a palette entry. The scenario's eight pixels show it: two boxes put one entry on the three dark greys and blues and one on the reds; four boxes separate the blues from the greys and the two reds from each other.

Then each pixel goes to its nearest entry, and the difference is error. Throwing the error away is threshold, and a ramp from black to white thresholded to two inks is a black half and a white half. Chapter 10's ordered dither compares each pixel with a Bayer threshold instead of 0.5, which spreads the error into a regular crosshatch. Floyd and Steinberg's error diffusion hands the error on: seven sixteenths to the pixel to the right and the rest to three pixels below, so a pixel that came out too dark makes its neighbours more likely to come out light. It keeps the image's light where it matters. On the ramp the error-diffused image averages 0.5007 in light against the ramp's 0.5; the ordered dither averages 0.5303. Do it in linear light, as this book does everything, and the average is right; most programs diffuse in encoded values, the chapter 1 mistake again, and their dithered mid-greys come out too light.

Writing the result needs a format that holds a palette, and PPM doesn't. The simplest one everything can open is the 8-bit BMP: a 14-byte file header, a 40-byte info header, 256 palette entries of four bytes each, blue first, and then one byte per pixel, the rows from the bottom up, each padded with zeros to a multiple of four bytes. There's no compression to write. The bottom-up rows and the padding are the two things to get wrong, and the byte-by-byte scenario checks both on a 5 by 2 image, whose rows are padded from five bytes to eight.

§ 25.4 Selection and undo

A selection is a coverage mask, chapter 12's clip under another name, and the selection tools are what chapter 12 already built. The rectangular marquee is clip_rect. Adding to a selection is the union of two masks, subtracting is a × (1 − b), intersecting is the product. The magic wand is the flood fill's mask. Feather blurs the mask, here with a box blur along the rows and then down the columns, which softens the edge without changing how much is selected: the feathered 4 by 4 square still adds up to 16.

Moving the selected pixels makes a floating selection: lift them out through the mask into a chapter 9 layer, premultiplied by the mask, fill the hole they leave with the background colour through the same mask, move the layer, and composite it back when it's dropped. Until then the float is a group, and you could fade it or blend it as chapter 12 would.

Undo is the feature that turned paint programs from toys into tools, and it's a stack. Before an edit runs, save the pixels of the rectangle it will change; undo puts them back, keeping what it replaced so that redo can put that back in turn; and any new edit after an undo throws the redo stack away, because the future it held no longer follows from the present. Saving only the rectangle is what makes it affordable: a brush dab touches a few hundred pixels, not the whole canvas. Real editors go one step further and save only the 64 by 64 tiles that changed, chapter 21's idea applied to memory, and share the unchanged ones between states.

§ 25.5 Putting it together

The scanline flood fill is the chapter's one algorithm that's easy to get subtly wrong, so it's the one to print.

flood_mask(c, x, y, tolerance, connectivity):
  seed ← bytes_at(c, x, y);  stack ← [(x, y)];  m ← all 0
  reach ← 1 if connectivity = 8 else 0
  while stack isn't empty:
    (px, py) ← pop the stack
    if m[px, py] = 1 or (px, py) doesn't match seed: continue
    lx ← px;  while lx > 0 and (lx − 1, py) is unmarked and matches: lx ← lx − 1
    rx ← px;  while rx < width − 1 and (rx + 1, py) is unmarked and matches: rx ← rx + 1
    m[lx .. rx, py] ← 1
    for ny in py − 1, py + 1 inside the canvas:
      inrun ← false
      for k from max(0, lx − reach) to min(width − 1, rx + reach):
        ok ← (k, ny) is unmarked and matches
        if ok and not inrun: push (k, ny)            // one seed per run
        inrun ← ok
  answer m
