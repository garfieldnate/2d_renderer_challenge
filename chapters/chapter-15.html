Chapter Fifteen

Dashes

Put eleven marks along a curve at t = 0, 0.1, 0.2, …, 1 and look at them. They bunch up at one end and spread out at the other, and not by a little: on the curve in Figure 15.1 the mark at t = 0.5 sits twenty-seven pixels short of halfway. A Bézier's parameter is not a distance. It never was; it's a recipe for blending control points, and it runs fast where the handles are long and crawls where they're short. Dashes have to be measured in distance, because a dash that's twelve pixels long on one part of a path and nine on another is the first thing anyone notices. So this chapter starts by finding out how long a curve is, which, like the offset, has no closed form. Then it cuts.

Where dashing sits matters as much as how it works. It is a path-to-path transform: a path goes in, a path of shorter open subpaths comes out, and chapter 13's stroker never learns it happened. Every dash gets caps like any other open subpath, every corner a dash turns gets a join, and the stroker doesn't grow a second job. The alternative, a stroker with a dash mode, is how you end up with a stroker nobody can read.

§ 15.1 How long is a curve

For a path it's trivial: add up the segments, and if the subpath is closed, add the one from the last point back to the first. That's path_length, and dashing a path needs nothing more. Curves are the problem. The length of a Bézier is the integral of its speed, and the speed is the square root of a polynomial, and that integral has no elementary answer. Chapter 8 crept up on it by flattening: the length of the polyline at tolerance 0.001 was 7.9992 for a curve whose true length is 8.0000. This chapter does the same thing on purpose and keeps the intermediate results, because the intermediate results are the useful part.

Evaluate the curve at n + 1 evenly spaced parameters, t = 0, 1/n, …, 1, and record the running length of the polyline through them: table[i] is the distance along the chords from the start to the point at t = i/n. That's the arc-length table, and its last entry is the curve's length, a little short because chords cut corners, and less short the bigger n is. With 256 chords the chapter 8 curve comes out at 7.99995. The table answers the question dashing needs asked: at what parameter is the running length s? Find the entry that spans it, which is a binary search, and interpolate linearly inside that one chord. Chords are straight, so on a chord the interpolation is exact; the only error is the chord's own. Before the start the answer is 0, past the end it's 1, and point_at_length and split_at_length are chapter 8's point_at and split_at handed the parameter the table found.

Figure 15.1 is the scenarios' lopsided() curve, cubic((15, 100), (25, 85), (100, 5), (185, 95)) in a 200 by 120 panel with marks of radius 3 (chapter 5's circles, 24 sides): one short handle, one long one, so marks at equal steps of t are four times as far apart at the end as at the start, and the midpoint by parameter lands at x = 71.9 while the midpoint by length is at 98.7. The scenarios pin the table on a curve small enough to check with a ruler, the convergence of its last entry as n grows, and the interpolation at a quarter and a half of the way along.

§ 15.2 The pattern

A dash pattern is a list of lengths that alternate on and off: [12, 6] is twelve on, six off, twelve on, and so on until the path runs out. Three rules about the list, all of them specified behaviour in every format that has dashes, and all of them the kind of thing only a scenario will make you get right. An odd number of entries is repeated: [5] means [5, 5], and [5, 2, 1] means [5, 2, 1, 5, 2, 1], so that the second cycle's on and off fall the same way as the first's. A pattern whose entries add up to zero, or an empty one, is no pattern: the path is drawn solid. And a negative entry makes the whole pattern invalid, which is also drawn solid. normalize_pattern turns the list you were given into the list the walk will use, and comes back empty for the two solid cases.

A zero-length entry, on the other hand, is not only legal but useful. A dash of length zero is a single point, and chapter 13 already knows what a single-point subpath is: under a round cap it's a dot, under a butt cap it's nothing. So [0, 9] with round caps is a dotted line, one dot every nine units, which is how every drawing program makes one. The scenarios pin all of it, including the fact that dash hands back the original subpaths when there is no pattern.

§ 15.3 The walk

Now cut. For each subpath, walk it from its start, segment by segment, keeping two things: which entry of the pattern you're in and how much of it is left. Take steps of the smaller of what's left in the entry and what's left in the segment. If the entry is an on, the step is part of a dash: start a new output subpath if one isn't open, and extend it to where the step ends. When the entry runs out, move to the next one, flip on to off or back, and close the dash you were building. When the segment runs out, move to the next segment without flipping anything: the walk goes straight through the vertices, so a dash that reaches a corner turns it and keeps its corner, and the stroker gives that corner a join. The phase is where in the pattern the walk begins: walk phase units into the pattern before you start, so a phase of 3 on [10, 5] starts seven units into the first dash. Take it modulo the pattern's sum first, so a phase of the whole sum is no phase at all, and a negative phase is the same as the sum plus it.

dash(path, pattern, phase):
  pat ← normalize_pattern(pattern)
  if pat is empty:  return a copy of path              // solid
  out ← path()
  for subpath in path:
    pts ← its points, plus its first point again if closed
    i ← 0;  remaining ← pat[0];  on ← true
    walk (phase mod sum(pat)) into the pattern, advancing i and flipping on
    dash ← none
    for each segment (a, b) of pts, skipping zero-length ones:
      pos ← 0
      while pos < |b − a|:
        step ← min(remaining, |b − a| − pos)
        if on:
          if dash is none:  dash ← move_to(out, a + (b − a)·pos/|b − a|)
          if step > 0:  line_to(out, a + (b − a)·(pos + step)/|b − a|)
        pos += step;  remaining −= step
        if remaining = 0:  i ← (i + 1) mod n;  remaining ← pat[i];  on ← not on;  dash ← none
    if subpath is closed and its last dash ends at its start and its first dash begins there:
      join the last dash to the first        // §15.4
  return out

Two details are decisions, and the scenarios pin them so your renderer and the book's agree. A dash that would begin exactly where the subpath ends is not emitted: [0, 6] on a line twelve long makes dots at 0 and 6, not at 12. And zero-length segments (chapter 13's duplicate points again) are skipped, which is what stops a zero step from looping forever. The rest is arithmetic, and the scenarios check the arithmetic: dashes on a straight line land at exact multiples, the dashes and the gaps add up to the length of the path, and the phase moves everything by exactly what it says.

§ 15.4 Closed subpaths, and starting over

The pattern starts over at every subpath, from the phase. That's the rule SVG and PDF both use, and it's the one you'd guess, but it means the same pattern on two subpaths of one path can disagree about where the dashes fall, and that's correct. A closed subpath is walked around its closing segment as well, back to the start, and then there's a corner case that every renderer handles and none of them advertise. If the walk is on when it gets back to the start, and it was on when it left, the last dash and the first dash are touching, and drawn separately they'd get two caps where there should be a join. So join them: the last dash absorbs the first, turning the starting corner as one subpath. If a single dash covers the whole loop, it comes back as the loop, closed. The square in the scenarios does all three: a pattern that ends on a gap and leaves the corner alone, one whose last dash runs into its first, and one dash longer than the perimeter.

The trap

A dash pattern is user data, and two of its legal forms will hang a walk written from intuition. An odd pattern that isn't doubled makes the second cycle start with an off where the first started with an on, so the dashes drift and nobody can say why. A pattern that adds up to zero, [0, 0] or [], never advances: a walk that takes steps of min(remaining, left) takes steps of zero forever. Both are specified behaviour, double and draw solid, and both are pinned, because the only way to find out your walk hangs is to hand it the pattern that hangs it.

§ 15.5 Putting it together

The plate needs a path where the parameter would have lied and the arc length doesn't, so it's a spiral: seven quarter circles, each φ times the radius of the last and tangent to it, which is the golden spiral you've seen drawn over nautilus shells. Each quarter is a cubic with the usual 0.5523 handles, and all seven are flattened into one open subpath, so the walk carries the pattern from the innermost turn to the outermost without a reset. Then dash it, and stroke every dash with a round cap, in the next of three inks. The dashes are the same length on the tight inner turns as on the wide outer sweep, which is the entire point, and the spiral itself is drawn faintly underneath so you can see what was cut.

golden_spiral():
  p ← path();  r ← 6;  center ← (148, 130);  θ ← π
  repeat 7 times:
    d0 ← (cos θ, sin θ);  d1 ← (cos(θ + π/2), sin(θ + π/2))
    P0 ← center + r·d0;  P3 ← center + r·d1
    flatten_into_path(p, cubic(P0, P0 + 0.5523·r·d1, P3 + 0.5523·r·d0, P3), 0.05)
    r' ← r·φ;  center ← P3 − r'·d1;  r ← r';  θ ← θ + π/2   // tangent-continuous
  return p

spiral_dashes():
  c ← canvas(340, 340), filled with paper
  stroke golden_spiral() 1 wide in dim gray
  for k, d in subpaths(dash(golden_spiral(), [16, 10], 0)):
    stroke d 7 wide, round caps, round joins, in INKS[k mod 3]
  return c

plate_15():
  return magnify(spiral_dashes(), 2)
