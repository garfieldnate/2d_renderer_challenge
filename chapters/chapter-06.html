Chapter Six

Filling a Polygon

Chapter 5's fill asks every pixel on the canvas whether it's inside, and to answer, every pixel walks every edge of the path. A 160-by-160 star costs 25,600 winding numbers, each over five edges, before you've antialiased anything. This chapter turns the question around: instead of asking each pixel about the edges, walk down the canvas one row at a time and ask each edge where it crosses the row. Same picture, pixel for pixel, seventy times faster in Python, and the gap grows with the canvas. The picture is still jagged. That's deliberate.

Here's the shape of the idea. A row of pixels is a horizontal line at some height. Where a polygon crosses that line, it crosses at a handful of points, and between the crossings the row is either entirely inside or entirely outside. So for each row: find the crossings, sort them, and fill the stretches that the winding number says are inside. Those stretches are called spans, and the whole algorithm is a sweep, top to bottom, emitting spans.

The scanline fill is older than raster displays, it's what every renderer did until the analytic one in chapter 7 took over, and its bones are still inside chapter 7. This chapter writes it the classical way, with an edge table and an active edge list, on purpose. Each part has a job, each part has a scenario, and the result has to be exactly the buffer chapter 5's center test produced, which is the best kind of test: a slow implementation you already trust, checking a fast one you don't yet.

§ 6.1 The edge table

Start by taking the path apart. Chapter 5's edges(p) gives you every edge of every subpath as a pair of points. For the sweep you want each edge in a more useful form: which of its two ends is higher on the canvas (smaller y), which is lower, where it crosses its own top, how far it moves in x for each unit it descends, and which way the path was going along it. Store those five numbers as y_top, y_bottom, x_top, slope and direction, with direction +1 when the path heads down the canvas along the edge, from a to b with a.y < b.y, and -1 when it heads up. That's the same sign chapter 5's winding number gave a crossing, and it's about to be used the same way.

edge_table(p) builds that for every edge that isn't horizontal and sorts the result by y_top, then by x_top. Ties beyond that are allowed to fall in any order; the sweep sorts crossings again on every row, so the table's order only has to be good enough to know which edges to bring in next. The tests reach into an entry with t[0].y_top and friends, and x_at(edge, y) is the one computation an edge knows how to do: x_top + (y - y_top) · slope.

Horizontal edges are dropped. Not clamped, not given a slope of zero and a height of one row, not special-cased into a span of their own: dropped. Chapter 5's half-open rule already said they never cross a sample height, and here there's a second reason, which is that their slope is a division by zero. A naive implementation that keeps them draws a spike out of every flat top, because the edge's x at the row is undefined and whatever it comes out as gets filled up to. If your first render of a rectangle has a hair sticking out of a corner, this is where it came from. Two scenarios check the flat-topped rectangle: two edges in the table, and a span on every row from the top to one before the bottom.

The trap

"Horizontal" means a.y = b.y exactly. Two vertices that should be at the same height and were computed by two different trigonometric calls can differ in the last bit: chapter 5's star has such a pair, at 58.86881039375369 and 58.86881039375366, and its edge table has five entries rather than the four you'd get on paper. The fifth edge has a slope of about -4.7 × 10¹⁵ and a height of 3 × 10⁻¹⁴, and it's harmless: no sample height will ever fall inside a span that thin, so it crosses nothing. It only matters if you were counting on the count. The scenarios don't pin the length of the star's table for exactly this reason, and neither should you. The rule is a.y = b.y, exactly, and what your sin does in the last bit is between you and your libm.

§ 6.2 Crossings on a row, and spans

Now one row. Pixel row r is sampled at its centers, height y = r + 0.5, the same place chapter 5's center test looked. An edge crosses that height when y_top ≤ y < y_bottom, half-open, lower end in and upper end out, which is chapter 5's rule again with the ends named. Where it crosses is x_at(edge, y). crossings_on_row(table, y) collects (x, direction) for every edge in the table that qualifies and sorts by x. This is the slow version: it looks at every edge for every row. §6.3 stops doing that.

From sorted crossings to spans is a walk. Start with winding 0 on the far left, which is outside. At each crossing add its direction. After each addition ask the rule, nonzero or even-odd, whether you're now inside. The moment the answer turns true, a span starts at that crossing; the moment it turns false, the span ends there. spans_from_crossings(xs, rule) returns the list of (x_start, x_end) pairs, as real numbers, merged: two inside stretches that touch are one span, because the rule never turned false between them. The scenarios hand it the same four crossings under both rules: nonzero sees one span, even-odd sees two with a gap, and that gap is the star's hole, one row at a time.

spans(p, rule, row) puts the two together for one pixel row, and it's the function the scenarios use to check the geometry without a buffer in the way. An axis-aligned rectangle from x = 1.25 to 4.75 has exactly that span on every row it covers, and no span on the row above or the row of its bottom edge. A triangle with its base on y = 0 and apex at (5, 10) has spans that narrow by exactly one pixel per row, 0.25 to 9.75 on row 0, 4.75 to 5.25 on row 9, and nothing on row 10, where the sample height of 10.5 is past the apex.

The last step turns a span into pixels. fill_span(cov, row, x0, x1) sets to 1 every pixel of the row whose center lies in [x0, x1): the first is ceil(x0 - 0.5), the last is ceil(x1 - 0.5) - 1, clipped to the buffer, and if the first is past the last there's nothing to do. Half-open at the right end, which is what makes two spans that meet at a pixel center fill that pixel exactly once. The span from 1.25 to 4.75 fills pixels 1 through 4, because their centers 1.5 through 4.5 are inside it. The span from 1.5 to 4.5 fills 1 through 3: 4.5 is the right end and the right end is out. A span may run off either side of the buffer or have no width at all, and there are scenarios for each, because clipping is where off-by-ones live.

spans_from_crossings(xs, rule):          // xs sorted by x, each (x, direction)
  out ← []
  w ← 0
  start ← none
  for (x, d) in xs:
    w ← w + d
    inside ← rule = "nonzero" ? w ≠ 0 : w is odd
    if inside and start is none:  start ← x
    if not inside and start is not none:  out.append((start, x));  start ← none
  return out

fill_span(cov, row, x0, x1):
  first ← ceil(x0 - 0.5)
  last  ← ceil(x1 - 0.5) - 1
  for x in max(first, 0)..min(last, cov.width - 1):     // inclusive; empty when first > last
    set_coverage(cov, x, row, 1)

§ 6.3 The sweep

You could fill a path now: for every row, crossings_on_row over the whole table, spans, fill_span. It would work and it would already be fast. But it checks every edge on every row, and a path with a thousand edges on a thousand-row canvas does a million checks to find the handful that matter. The classical fix is the active edge list, and it's what makes this a renderer rather than a demo.

The table is sorted by y_top. Sweep the rows from the top. Keep a list of the edges that are currently active, meaning they span the current sample height. At each row, two things change: edges whose y_top has been reached join the list, and since the table is sorted you take them off its front without searching; and edges whose y_bottom has been passed leave it. Everything else in the list stays. Then compute x_at for each active edge, sort those crossings, and fill the spans. The table is read once, front to back, and every row touches only the edges that cross it:

fill_path_aliased(p, rule, w, h):
  cov    ← coverage_buffer(w, h)
  table  ← edge_table(p)                       // sorted by y_top
  active ← []
  next   ← 0                                   // index of the first edge not yet activated
  for row in 0..h-1:
    y ← row + 0.5
    while next < length(table) and table[next].y_top ≤ y:
      active.append(table[next]);  next ← next + 1
    active ← [e for e in active if e.y_bottom > y]     // half-open: out once y reaches the bottom
    xs ← [(x_at(e, y), e.direction) for e in active], sorted by x
    for (x0, x1) in spans_from_crossings(xs, rule):
      fill_span(cov, row, x0, x1)
  return cov

Two remarks about the sort. Sorting the crossings on each row is the honest simple version. Renderers that care keep the active list ordered by x and re-sort it with one pass of insertion sort, because between one row and the next the order almost never changes: edges swap places only where they cross, and a polygon's edges rarely do. You can do that here if you like; nothing in the scenarios can tell. The other remark is the one that matters. The edge that starts exactly at a sample height, y_top = y, is active on that row, and the edge that ends there, y_bottom = y, is not. That's the half-open rule for the third time, and it's what makes the sweep agree with chapter 5's winding_at at every pixel center, including the ones that sit on a vertex. A rectangle from y = 2.5 to 5.5 has its edges exactly on sample heights, and the scenario for it fills rows 2, 3 and 4 and not row 5; get either comparison wrong and it fills four rows or two.

Which brings you to the test. fill_path_aliased(p, rule, w, h) returns a coverage buffer of zeros and ones. Chapter 5's rasterize_centers(filled(p, rule), w, h) returns a coverage buffer of zeros and ones. They are the same buffer, for every path, under either rule, and the scenarios say so with max_coverage_difference(a, b) = 0, a helper that's chapter 1's max_channel_difference for coverage buffers: the largest difference between corresponding entries, or 1 when the sizes differ, because two buffers of different sizes can't be the same picture. It has a scenario of its own, including the size case, so that a transposed sweep can't compare equal to anything.

Run the star through both and time them. On this machine, in Python, the sweep takes about a millisecond and the center test about seventy: the ratio is the number of edges times the number of pixels that don't touch them, and it only grows. In a compiled language the sweep is too fast to time with a stopwatch. The scenario compares them on a rectangle, a triangle, a polygon circle at an awkward offset, and the star under both rules, and every one comes out identical. If yours doesn't, the first place to look is the two half-open comparisons in the sweep and the two in fill_span; a ≤ where a < belongs shows up as one extra row or one extra column, and the diff will tell you which.

    And   max_coverage_difference(nz, rasterize_centers(filled(p, "nonzero"), 20, 20)) = 0

§ 6.4 Paths through matrices

One small function has been waiting since chapter 4. transform_path(p, m) is a new path with every point of every subpath taken through m, closed flags and all; it's transform_points with the subpath structure kept. The original is untouched, and a scenario checks that, because the alternative, transforming in place, is the kind of thing that works until you draw the same path twice. With it, a path you've described once can be drawn anywhere, at any size, at any angle, which is the whole reason chapter 4 happened.

The pen question from chapter 4's trap doesn't come up here: a fill has no pen. Transform the points, fill the result, and the picture is right. Strokes, in chapter 13, will have to decide.

In the GUI

View → Pixel Preview in Illustrator, and the Antialiasing checkbox in Inkscape's Document Properties, switched off. Both show you this chapter's output: the shape filled by pixel centers, hard-edged. Designers use it to see what an icon will do at 16 pixels, which is a question chapter 7's smooth edges answer less honestly.

§ 6.5 Putting it together

Something that would have taken chapter 5 all afternoon. Take the star, move it to the origin and shrink it to radius 1, so that one matrix can put it anywhere at any size: unit_star() is transform_path(star(), scaling(1/70, 1/70) * translation(-80.5, -80.5)). Then twenty-four of them along a spiral, each one bigger, each one turned a little further, in three inks, filled nonzero by the sweep into a 320-by-320 canvas and magnified by 2:

unit_star():
  return transform_path(star(), scaling(1 / 70, 1 / 70) * translation(-80.5, -80.5))

spiral():
  c ← canvas(320, 320)
  fill(c, color(0.02, 0.02, 0.025))
  inks ← [color(0.9, 0.55, 0.1), color(0.2, 0.55, 0.85), color(0.85, 0.25, 0.3)]
  for k in 0..23:
    a ← k * 25 degrees
    r ← 20 + 5 * k
    m ← translation(160.5 + r * cos(a), 160.5 + r * sin(a)) * rotation(a) * scaling(6 + 1.25 * k, 6 + 1.25 * k)
    cov ← fill_path_aliased(transform_path(unit_star(), m), "nonzero", 320, 320)
    paint_through(c, cov, inks[k mod 3])
  return c

plate_06():
  return magnify(spiral(), 2)

Read the matrix right to left, as chapter 4 taught you: scale the unit star up, turn it, move it out along the spiral. Every star is the same five points. The scenarios pin the unit star's corners and bounds, then probe one pixel in the middle of a star of each ink and one in an arm of the biggest, then diff the whole picture. If your spiral winds the other way, your rotation turns the wrong way, and chapter 4's F would have told you.
