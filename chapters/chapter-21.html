Chapter Twenty-One

Making It Fast

The book's Python reference implementation took 29 seconds to draw the tiger. For most of that time it was adding zero to zero. Every fill in chapter 20 builds an accumulator the size of the canvas, and every one of the tiger's 305 fills and strokes resolves all 202,500 of its cells, including the whiskers that cover a few dozen. Then it paints: it walks every pixel of the canvas again to find the ones with coverage, and along the way it blends in 850,000 pixels whose coverage is around 0.0000000000000001, which is not a number any eye has ever seen. None of that is wrong. The picture is right. This chapter makes the same picture, byte for byte, resolving one cell in seventy-five, and the hardest part is proving it's the same picture.

The rule of this chapter is the one every performance engineer learns by being wrong: measure first. The outline this book was planned from said that tiling the canvas would be the single biggest win. It isn't, and you'll know that for sure by § 21.3, because you'll have counted. The second rule is that the chapter is only safe because of every golden image before it. Each optimization here changes the arithmetic somewhere, and the reference images are what tell you whether it changed the picture.

§ 21.1 Counting the work

Wall-clock time is the thing you care about and the worst thing to test. It depends on the machine, the language, the compiler, and what the browser in the next window is doing, and a scenario that pins it fails the day you buy a laptop. So count work instead, in units that don't depend on any of that. A stats() record holds three counters. cells is the number of accumulator cells a running sum resolved, which is what the fill costs. blends is the number of pixels composited one at a time, a paint sample and a source-over each, which is what painting costs. copies is the number of pixels written without a blend, which is a cheaper thing that doesn't exist yet.

fill_path_counted is chapter 7's fill with a line added: cells grows by the width times the height of the canvas, because that's what chapter 7 resolves. draw_coverage_counted is chapter 20's draw_coverage counting every pixel it blends. And render_svg_with(text, width, height, mode, st) is chapter 20's walker with every fill and stroke done one of three ways, counting as it goes. Mode "whole" is chapter 20 exactly as you wrote it. The other two modes are the next two sections. Keep a stopwatch on it too, for your own interest; the book quotes the reference implementation's times as a rough guide and never tests them.

§ 21.2 Bounds

A path can't touch a pixel outside its bounds. That's the whole idea, and chapter 12's trap already said it about group buffers. fill_bounds is the window of whole pixels a path can reach: from the floor of its smallest x and y to one past the floor of its largest, cut to the canvas. One past the floor, not the ceiling, because an edge lying exactly on x = 20 deposits into cell 20, and cell 20 had better be in the window. fill_path_bounded moves the path so the window's corner is the origin, fills it into an accumulator the window's size, and answers a window: that coverage buffer and where it sits on the canvas. draw_window paints only the window's pixels. Nothing about the fill changes but its size.

That's about ten lines, and it takes the tiger from 61,762,500 cells to 1,395,287. Forty-four times less work, from ten lines, and the Python reference goes from 29 seconds to under two. The blends halve as well, and that's the stranger number: the whole-canvas fill was painting all those pixels of coverage 10−16, the crumbs a running sum leaves to the right of every shape when its pluses and minuses don't quite cancel in floating point. A bounded fill never visits those pixels, so it never paints the crumbs.

One thing did change, and it's small enough that you'd never find it without a test built to look. Moving the path by −x0 changes the numbers accumulate adds together: xa + xb at x = 400 rounds differently from the same sum at x = 3. The coverage comes out different in the fifteenth decimal place, which is why the scenario compares with a tolerance of 0.000001 and not with equality, and why the bytes, which are what the reader of a PPM gets, don't change at all.

§ 21.3 Tiles

Inside the window there's still waste. A big shape, the harbor's sky, is solid across almost all of its window, and resolving a cell deep inside it produces the number 1 by adding up zeros. Cut the canvas into tiles sixteen pixels square and ask of each one, before resolving a single cell of it, whether the answer can be anything but constant.

The accumulator already knows. A tile that no edge deposited into has nothing in its cells, so every cell's winding is the running sum arriving at the tile's left edge, and that sum is the covers of the cells to its left, which you can add up without resolving anything. If the sum arriving on every row of the tile is the same whole number, the tile is uniform: solid when that number fills under the rule, empty when it doesn't. If an edge deposited into the tile, or the rows disagree, the tile is partial, and only partial tiles get their cells resolved. The rows can disagree with no deposit in sight, because a horizontal edge deposits nothing: the bottom of the harbor's hills runs straight along y = 212, through the middle of a row of tiles, and a classifier that only looked at each tile's top row painted those tiles solid down to y = 223. "Within 0.000001 of a whole number" is the test, because the sum arrives with crumbs of its own.

Store the accumulator sparsely while you're at it, as a map from cell to its two numbers, because most cells are never deposited into and the classifier only needs the ones that were. fill_path_tiled answers a tiled coverage: the class of every tile and the resolved cells of the partial ones. For the tiger it resolves 816,480 cells, down from 1,395,287. That's a factor of 1.7, which is good and is not forty-four. The tiger is hundreds of small shapes, most of which are all edge, and tiling pays off in proportion to how much interior a drawing has: the harbor, which is mostly sky and sea, drops by 2.8. The outline's prediction was wrong, and counting is how you find out before you spend a week on the wrong thing.

§ 21.4 Spans

A solid tile is sixteen rows of sixteen pixels that all have coverage 1, and painting pixel by pixel throws that away. Paint it as runs instead, and two things fall out. A solid paint's colour is the same at every pixel, so look it up once for the tile instead of calling paint_at 256 times. And when the paint is a solid colour at alpha 1, the blend is c·1 + (1 − 1)·d, which is c exactly, so don't blend: write the colour. draw_tiled does that. It isn't a trick with a tolerance; the copy is bit for bit what the blend would have produced, and the scenario checks it with layers_equal, which compares every channel of every pixel with =.

For the tiger that turns 207,872 blends into copies, 812 solid tiles of 256 pixels each. It's the cheapest line of code in the chapter and in a compiled language it's a memcpy.

§ 21.5 Four pixels at a time

After tiling, the hottest loop left is the one that composites a colour into a row of pixels through a run of coverages. For every pixel, four channels, each c·k + (1 − k)·d: no branches, no dependency from one pixel to the next. That is the exact shape a vector unit wants, and most languages have a way to ask for one, whether that's Rust's std::simd, C#'s System.Numerics.Vector<T>, Java's Vector API or NumPy. (Rust's is still unstable; four-element arrays in a loop get most of the way on stable.)

// scalar: one pixel, four channels, per step
for i in 0 .. n − 1:
  k ← ks[i];  t ← 1 − k
  d ← row[x + i]
  row[x + i] ← (c.r·k + t·d.r,  c.g·k + t·d.g,  c.b·k + t·d.b,  k + t·d.a)

// four wide: one register holds one channel of four pixels
for i in 0, 4, 8, … while i + 4 ≤ n:
  k ← load4(ks, i);  t ← 1 − k
  r ← c.r·k + t·load4(row.r, x + i)          // multiply, then add: never fused
  g ← c.g·k + t·load4(row.g, x + i)
  b ← c.b·k + t·load4(row.b, x + i)
  a ← k + t·load4(row.a, x + i)
  store4 r, g, b, a
then the last n mod 4 pixels one at a time, as the scalar loop

Three honest notes. First, the scalar loop has no if k > 0, and doesn't need one: with k = 0 the formula is c·0 + 1·d, which is d exactly, so a vector lane with nothing to paint writes back what it read. Second, "never fused" matters. A fused multiply-add rounds once where the scalar code rounds twice, and many compilers and vector libraries will fuse for you if you let them. The result is more accurate and different, and the golden images will say so. composite_span4 has to do exactly the scalar arithmetic in each lane, and the scenario checks it against composite_span with layers_equal. Third, expect a real speedup on this loop and much less on the whole render. The loop is only part of the time, and Amdahl has not gone anywhere.

If your language has no portable vector type, the lesson survives: write the loop so the compiler can vectorize it for you. Contiguous channels, a trip count known at the top, no aliasing between what you read and what you write, no branch inside. That's the loop above.

§ 21.6 What changed, and what didn't

The trap

Every optimization in this chapter changes rounding somewhere. The bounded fill moves the path, and xa + xb rounds differently near the origin: the triangle in the bounds scenario differs from chapter 7's coverage by 0.0000000000000028. The whole-canvas fill left 705 cells of that triangle's canvas at about 0.00000000000000008 where the bounded fill has 0, and painted every one. A solid tile says 1 where resolving it can say 0.9999999999999998. A vector loop that fuses its multiply and add rounds once instead of twice. Each of these is too small to see, and a renderer that tests its coverage with = would call every one of them a failure. A renderer with no golden images would never find out that one of them, some day, isn't too small to see. So test the bytes, which is what the reader of a PPM gets, with a budget of 0 between the modes; test the coverage with a tolerance written in the open; and run every render the book has made before you believe any change here. That suite is the reason this chapter could be attempted at all.

Not everything worth doing got done. Clips and groups still use canvas-sized buffers, and sizing a group's layer to its bounds, as chapter 12 promised, is the same ten lines as § 21.2 applied to layers. Tiles are independent, so they parallelize. The flattened path could be cached between the fill and the stroke. And the accumulator could be integer. Production rasterizers from FreeType's on store coordinates in fixed point, 24.8 or 26.6: an integer with eight or six bits of fraction. Additions become exact, the running sum returns to exactly zero at the right of every shape, and every crumb in the trap goes away, at the price of snapping every coordinate to 1/256 of a pixel and choosing what happens at the overflow edge. The book kept floats from chapter 1 because they're what the reader already thinks in, and they turned out to cost nothing but a tolerance.

§ 21.7 Putting it together

The tiled fill and its painter, together. They're the chapter's thirty lines, and they are most of the win.

fill_path_tiled(p, rule, width, height, st):
  x0, y0, x1, y1 ← fill_bounds(p, width, height);  classes ← every tile "empty"
  if the window is empty: return classes
  cells ← chapter 7's deposits for every edge of p, kept in a map by (x, row)
  for every tile (tx, ty) inside the window:
    arriving[tx, r] ← covers of the deposited cells left of tx × 16 in row r, for each row r of the tile
    if a cell in the tile holds a nonzero area or cover: classes[ty][tx] ← "partial"
    else:
      n ← round(arriving[tx, first row of the tile])
      if some row has |arriving[tx, r] − n| > 0.000001: classes[ty][tx] ← "partial"
      else: classes[ty][tx] ← "solid" if apply_rule(n, rule) = 1 else "empty"
  for every partial tile, for every row r of it:
    running ← arriving[tx, r]
    for every column x of it:
      area, cover ← cells[x, r], or 0, 0
      value[x, r] ← apply_rule(running + area, rule);  running ← running + cover
      st.cells ← st.cells + 1
  return classes and value

draw_tiled(l, t, paint, alpha, st):
  for every tile not "empty", for every pixel (x, y) of it inside the canvas:
    if the tile is "solid" and paint is a solid colour c and alpha = 1:
      l[x, y] ← (c.r, c.g, c.b, 1);  st.copies ← st.copies + 1
    else:
      k ← (1 if "solid" else value[x, y]) × alpha
      if k > 0: l[x, y] ← the paint at (x + 0.5, y + 0.5), at k, over l[x, y];  st.blends ← st.blends + 1

And the measurements, every mode on all three of chapter 20's documents, every render byte-identical to chapter 20's. The times are the Python reference's on one laptop, and they are the least interesting column; the Python times flatten out once painting is all that's left, because a Python blend is expensive, where a compiled one is a few instructions. The cells and copies are exact for any implementation that follows the scenarios. The blends are not: they count the crumbs too, pixels with coverage around 10−16, and whether a crumb is 0 or not depends on the last bit of the geometry, so only the tiger's are pinned and yours may differ by a few dozen elsewhere. Feature: Plate 21
  Every mode of render_svg_with draws exactly the bytes chapter 20's
  render_svg draws. Only fills and strokes count; the fills that build a
  clip's coverage are not counted, and a shape with a clip has its window
  or tiles turned into a canvas-sized coverage, multiplied by the clip and
  painted with draw_coverage_counted. tile_work(text, width, height) renders the document
  in the "tiled" mode and, for every tile, counts how many of its fills and
  strokes classified that tile partial and how many solid: tile_work[ty][tx]
  is the pair (partial, solid). work_map() is 910 by 450 paper: the tiger
  as the "tiled" walker draws it at (0, 0), and from x = 460 one square per
  tile of it: the pixels whose x mod 16 or y mod 16 is 0 or 15 are left
  as paper, so each square is its tile's 16 pixels inset by one on every
  side and then cut by the canvas; the rest of the tile is magenta mixed into paper at
  0.15 + 0.85 × partial / the largest partial count of any tile when the
  tile's partial count is above 0; cyan mixed in at 0.6 when it's 0 and
  its solid count isn't; paper when both are 0. The mixes are in linear
  light, with chapter 16's inks. plate_21() is work_map().
