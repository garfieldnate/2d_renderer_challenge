Chapter One

The Canvas and the Color

Here's a question you'd think a graphics book could answer in one line. You have a black pixel and a white pixel. What number is exactly halfway between them?

If you said 128, you're in excellent company. That's what almost every piece of software written before about 2010 said too, and plenty written since. It's also wrong, in a way that will quietly bend the color of every gradient, every antialiased edge and every soft shadow you draw for the rest of this book.

The right answer is 188. Not because of some obscure convention nobody follows, but because of how the numbers in an image file relate to actual light, and the two are nothing like proportional. Before you take my word for it, look at this.

The checkerboard is the honest half. Every other pixel is off, every other pixel is fully on, so the light coming off that patch is exactly 50% of full white. And it matches the patch on the right, the one labeled 188. The patch labeled 128, the one everybody's arithmetic produces, is visibly darker.

By the end of this chapter you'll have a canvas, a way to get pictures out of your program, and a renderer that knows the difference. You'll also have rendered that figure yourself, from your own code, and stared at it in your own image viewer. It's a short chapter. The trap in the middle of it is the reason the rest of the book works.

§ 1.1 How to read a test

Every step in this book comes with a test, written in Gherkin, the plain-English format that Cucumber runs. You don't need Cucumber. The scenarios are short enough to translate into whatever test framework your language already has, and plenty of readers do exactly that. What you do need is to agree with me on what the notation means, so here it is, once.

  • c ← color(1, 0, 0) assigns. Read the arrow as "gets".
  • a = b between two numbers means within 0.0001 of each other, never bit-for-bit identical. Floating point arithmetic doesn't do identical. When a scenario needs a different tolerance it writes it in the open: a = b ± 0.00001. The same goes for ≠.
  • A color equals a color when each of its three numbers does. A triple of whole numbers like (0, 188, 255) is a pixel as it appears in a file, and those compare exactly unless a ± 1 says otherwise, in which case each of the three may be off by one.
  • Operators stand for whatever your language does. If it has operator overloading, c1 + c2 is an overloaded plus. If it doesn't, it's a function, and the book's names for them are add, subtract, scale for a color times a number and multiply for a color times a color. Some typed languages refuse c * 2 because the literal wants to become a color; write c * 2.0 or use scale.
  • a ≤ b, and = between two whole numbers such as a count, are exact.
  • line 4 of ppm is followed by a quoted string compares that one line exactly, and ppm ends with a newline character means what it says.
  • The names in the scenarios, write_pixel, canvas_to_ppm and so on, are the names the tests call. Rename them if your language's conventions insist, but rename them in your step definitions too, and don't rename them casually: later chapters refer back to them.
  • round(x) rounds to the nearest whole number. No test in this book lands close enough to a half for the tie rule to matter, and the file comparisons allow a difference of one anyway, so any tie-breaking rule your language ships with is acceptable. I checked.
  • every pixel of c is some color, and exactly N pixels of c are some color, are loops over the whole canvas comparing with the usual tolerance. The count is exact.
  • lines 4-5 of ppm are followed by a quoted block compares those lines of the text, one by one, with the lines of the block. The block is indented to sit inside the scenario; strip that indentation before comparing.
  • The short programs printed in the text are pseudo-code, and their ranges are inclusive at both ends: 0..99 is a hundred values. Watch for that if your language's ranges stop one short.
  • A named program printed in the text, like gray_match(), is a function the scenarios call by that name. Write it as printed, returning what it says it returns.
  • A Scenario Outline is a template. Each row of its Examples table is one test, with the row's values dropped into the <placeholders>.

The first three scenarios test nothing but the notation. Write the comparison helper your tests will use for the rest of the book and make sure it agrees with them. JUnit's assertEquals(expected, actual, delta), pytest's approx and Rust's approx crate are all this function under other names, and it's fine to use one of them. It's also fine to write it yourself, because it's one line.

§ 1.2 A color is three numbers

Start with the smallest thing. A color is a triple of red, green and blue, and you're going to keep them as floating point numbers where 0.0 is none and 1.0 is full, rather than as bytes from 0 to 255.

Two reasons. The first is that you'll be adding, scaling and averaging colors constantly, and doing that in bytes means rounding at every step until your grays turn brown. The second is that values are allowed to wander outside the 0-to-1 range in the middle of a calculation, and that's fine. You clamp once, at the very end, when the pixels are on their way out to a file. A color of 1.7 isn't a bug. It's a bright thing you haven't finished computing yet.

One more thing about those three numbers, and it's the thing this chapter is about: they measure light. A color of 0.5 is half as many photons as a color of 1.0. Hold onto that for a few pages, because it's going to turn out that the numbers in image files don't work that way, and the gap between the two is where every gradient you've ever seen went wrong.

You need four operations, and you already know all of them:

  • Add and subtract, component by component. Adding light to light.
  • Scale by a number. Half as bright, twice as bright.
  • Multiply two colors together, component by component: red times red, green times green, blue times blue. This is the Hadamard product, and it's what you use when one color filters another. A green light through a red gel gives you nothing at all, which the arithmetic gets right without being told.

§ 1.3 The canvas

A canvas is a rectangle of colors with a width, a height, and two operations: put a color at a position, and read the color back. Every pixel starts black.

The only decision worth pausing on is which way is up, and the answer is that it isn't. Your canvas has its origin at the top left, x increasing to the right and y increasing downward. That's upside down from every graph you drew in school, and it's what image files, screens, printers and every 2D API in existence agree on. Fighting it costs you a sign error in every chapter. Accept it now.

Column first, row second. There is a scenario below whose entire purpose is to catch you swapping them, because everyone does it once and the picture that results, a transposed render, looks almost plausible.

Writes outside the canvas are ignored. Not an error, not a wraparound, not a resize. Silently dropped. By chapter 6 you'll have geometry hanging off every edge, and you do not want a bounds check throwing in the middle of a scanline. The test pins all four sides, which means x and y can be negative: give them a signed integer type, even in a language whose array indices aren't. Reads outside the canvas are left undefined, because nothing in the book ever does one; if yours throws, good.

The last operation, fill, sets every pixel to one color. You'll use it for backgrounds from here to the end of the book. The pseudo-code in this book writes write_pixel and fill as statements that change the canvas in place. In a language without mutable values, have them return the new canvas and assign it back; nothing else changes.

§ 1.4 The numbers are not the light

Now the part that matters.

When an image file says 128, it is not asking the screen for half as much light as 255. It's asking for about 21.6% as much. The value in the file has been through a transfer function, a deliberate, standardized bend, and undoing that bend is called decoding. Applying it is encoding.

The reason the bend exists is that your eye is not a light meter. It's far more sensitive to changes among dark tones than among bright ones. If you spread 256 evenly spaced light levels across a gradient, the dark end would band horribly while the bright end wasted dozens of values on differences you can't see. So the encoding spends more of its 256 values down in the shadows, where you'll notice, and fewer up in the highlights, where you won't. It's a compression scheme for human eyeballs, and it's been so successful that most programmers never learn it's there.

The standard your images are in is sRGB. It's two functions, both operating on numbers between 0 and 1, and you can copy them:

// file value  →  light
decode(v) = v ≤ 0.04045
              ? v / 12.92
              : ((v + 0.055) / 1.055) ^ 2.4

// light  →  file value
encode(l) = l ≤ 0.0031308
              ? l * 12.92
              : 1.055 * l ^ (1 / 2.4) - 0.055

The little linear segment near zero isn't decoration. A pure power curve has infinite slope at black, and the straight piece keeps the numbers well behaved there. Copy it exactly, thresholds included. The scenarios test on both sides of both thresholds, because the classic bug is getting the branch backwards and only noticing three chapters later when your shadows look wrong. Both functions are defined on numbers between 0 and 1 and nothing else; every value in the tables was chosen with room to spare inside the tolerance, so a marginal failure is a real one.

You'll also want both functions on colors, meaning applied to each of the three numbers in turn. When the chapter writes encode(a) for a color a, that's what it means.

Now run the numbers from the top of the chapter. Decode 128/255 and you get 0.2159: that patch is emitting 21.6% of full light. Encode 0.5, genuine, physical, half-the-photons light, and you get 0.7354, which is 188 out of 255. The checkerboard in Figure 1.1 was never lying. Your arithmetic was.

§ 1.5 Getting it out of the computer

A canvas nobody can see isn't worth much. You need a file format, and you're going to use the least sophisticated one ever standardized: PPM.

PPM is a text file. It has three header lines, the magic string P3, the width and height, and the maximum channel value, and then the pixel values as decimal numbers, left to right, top to bottom. That's the entire specification. A three-by-one canvas with a red pixel, a half-light green pixel and a dim blue one comes out like this:

P3
3 1
255
255 0 0 0 188 0 0 0 128

You can open that in a text editor. You can read it out loud. When your renderer produces something baffling in chapter 7, being able to cat the output and stare at actual numbers is worth more than any debugger, and it's why you're starting here instead of with PNG.

Look at the green pixel. The canvas held 0.5, and the file says 188, not 128. That's the whole architecture of this renderer in one number: the canvas holds light, and the PPM writer encodes. Nothing else in the book ever thinks about the transfer function again. Every gradient, every blend, every antialiased edge does its arithmetic on light, and the one function that writes files does the bend on the way out.

Pixels go into the file in the order you'd read a page: the top row left to right, then the next row, down to the bottom. Converting a channel to a file value is four steps, in this order:

  1. Clamp to 0..1. A color of 1.7 becomes 1.0; a color of -0.5 becomes 0.
  2. Encode.
  3. Multiply by 255.
  4. Round to the nearest whole number.

Clamp before you encode. encode is only defined on 0..1, and while its linear branch happens to keep negatives away from pow in most languages, so you'd get away with clamping late, the definition clamps first and a language whose pow rejects a negative base won't forgive you. Round at the end, not truncate, or every value in your files will be off by one in the same direction and every test with a reference image will fail by exactly that much.

One oddity to respect: some very old PPM readers choke on lines longer than 70 characters. The rule you'll implement is greedy word packing. Emit channel values one at a time, separated by single spaces; before adding one, if the line would end up longer than 70 characters counting the space, that is if length(line) + 1 + length(value) > 70, start a new line instead. Exactly 70 is fine, and a pixel's three numbers may end up split across two lines. Each row of pixels starts on a fresh line regardless. Two scenarios pin it, one where a ten-pixel row breaks and one whose first line lands on exactly 70 characters, so there's nothing to guess. It's a strange little constraint to be honoring in the third decade of the twenty-first century, and it takes four lines of code, and if you skip it the one tool you eventually need will be the one that cares.

Three more functions in this section, and they exist so that tests can read files back. None is a renderer function; each is about five lines, and all three start the same way: split the text on whitespace, read the width and height out of the second and third tokens, and treat everything after the fourth token as pixel data. ppm_pixel(ppm, x, y) returns the three whole numbers at pixel (x, y), which is where the width comes in. distinct_values(ppm) returns how many different channel numbers appear in the pixel data: a file whose pixels are all 255 0 0 and 0 188 0 has four. max_channel_difference(a, b) takes two files and returns the largest absolute difference between any pair of corresponding pixel numbers, headers excluded, with one rule on top: if the two files don't have the same width and height, either one, the answer is 255. Without that rule a transposed render compares as a perfect match, and a transposed render is the one mistake this chapter has already warned you about. Later in this chapter you'll compare your output against the book's reference images with this function, and every later chapter does too. All three take the file's text, so the obvious implementation re-splits it on every call; if a later chapter's suite gets slow, parse once and keep the numbers.

§ 1.6 Your first picture

Now render Figure 1.1 yourself. Not because you doubt me, although you should, but because seeing it come out of your own code is the moment this chapter stops being trivia.

The picture is 300 by 100. The left third is a checkerboard: a pixel is white when x + y is even and black otherwise. The middle third is solid, at the light that a file value of 128 stands for, which is decode(128 / 255). The right third is solid at half light, 0.5. Wrap it in a function called gray_match that returns the canvas, so the tests can call it.

gray_match():
  c ← canvas(300, 100)
  for y in 0..99:
    for x in 0..99:
      write_pixel(c, x, y, (x + y) even ? color(1, 1, 1) : color(0, 0, 0))
  g ← decode(128 / 255)
  for y in 0..99:
    for x in 100..199:
      write_pixel(c, x, y, color(g, g, g))
  for y in 0..99:
    for x in 200..299:
      write_pixel(c, x, y, color(0.5, 0.5, 0.5))
  return c

That 128 / 255 is a division of two real numbers, not two integers. In a language where 128 / 255 is 0, write 128.0 / 255.0, and watch for the same trap in every x / 255 to come.

The test for this one counts, as well as looking: a hundred by a hundred checkerboard has exactly 5000 white pixels, and the two gray patches contribute none, so 5000 pixels of the whole canvas are white. Counting pixels of a given color is a loop, not a renderer function, and it catches the mistake where a pattern is right along one edge and wrong everywhere else.

Write it to a file, open it at 100% zoom, and lean back. The checkerboard should melt into the right-hand patch, the 188 one. If it melts into the left one instead, your PPM writer is scaling instead of encoding, and the middle scenario in the previous section should already be red.

Looking at a PPM

Not every image viewer opens PPM, and the one that ships with your operating system may not. GIMP, IrfanView, feh and most Linux viewers do. If yours doesn't, ImageMagick converts one in a second: magick gray-match.ppm gray-match.png. Whatever you use, view it at 100%. A viewer that scales the image resamples the checkerboard and the comparison is off.

Then push on it. A checkerboard is half the light because half the pixels are on. If one pixel in four is on, that's a quarter of the light, and it should match a solid patch of color(0.25, 0.25, 0.25), whose file value is 137. The pattern that puts one pixel in four on is: white when x + y is a multiple of 4, and 0 counts as a multiple. Call it quarter_match:

quarter_match():
  c ← canvas(200, 100)
  for y in 0..99:
    for x in 0..99:
      write_pixel(c, x, y, (x + y) mod 4 = 0 ? color(1, 1, 1) : color(0, 0, 0))
  for y in 0..99:
    for x in 100..199:
      write_pixel(c, x, y, color(0.25, 0.25, 0.25))
  return c

You'll notice the second match is a little harder to see than the first. That's honest too. A display doesn't reproduce the darks perfectly, and your eye isn't a light meter either. It's close enough to convince you, and the numbers in the test are exact regardless.

The last step of each scenario compares your file to the book's, allowing each number to differ by one. That one is for rounding: different languages' pow functions disagree in the last few bits, and once in a while that flips a rounding. Anything larger than one is a bug on one side or the other, and you should assume it's yours until you've checked. I've been wrong before, but I've run these.

§ 1.7 Two ways to mix

Here's the whole lesson in one operation. To blend two colors, a fraction t of the way from a to b, you have a choice:

// the way that feels obvious and is wrong
a' ← clamp(a),  b' ← clamp(b)
mix(a, b, t) = decode( encode(a') + (encode(b') - encode(a')) * t )

// the way light actually works
mix(a, b, t) = a + (b - a) * t

Both are component-wise, red with red and so on. The second one is a plain linear interpolation on the light values your colors already hold. The first one encodes both ends into file values, interpolates those, and decodes the result back into light so it can go on the canvas. The decode at the end isn't a typo: the canvas holds light, so the PPM writer will encode this color again on the way out, and the file value you get is the interpolated file value. That's what you get if you build your renderer on bytes and never think about it, and it's what every web browser does when you write a CSS gradient. The clamps are there because encode is only defined on 0..1 and canvas colors aren't. They have to be on the ends, before encoding, not on the result: clamping the result gives a different, wrong midpoint, and there's a scenario that tells the two apart.

Decode into light, do the arithmetic there, encode on the way out. That's the entire correction, and because your canvas already holds light, the correct version is the one with less code in it. You'll apply it for the rest of the book without thinking about it.

The trap

Doing this correctly will make your output look different from your browser. Web browsers, most design tools and a decent share of image editors blend in encoded space, the wrong way, because they've always done it and changing now would alter billions of existing pages. Your red-to-green gradient will pass through a bright olive. Chrome's will pass through a muddy brown. Yours is right, and it will look wrong to you for about a week.

So put a switch in. One global boolean, linear blending, defaulting to on. When it's off, mix does the browser thing. When something you render doesn't match a reference image from another tool, flipping that switch tells you in one second whether you've found a real bug or a philosophical disagreement. You'll use it more than you expect.

The switch is global state, and global state in tests needs one rule: every scenario that doesn't say otherwise expects it on. Reset it before each scenario, or turn it back on at the end of any scenario that turns it off. If your test runner runs tests in parallel by default, as Rust's and some JUnit setups do, a plain global will leak between scenarios and fail at random; make it thread-local or run those tests serially. If your language objects to a mutable global on principle, as Swift 6 and every pure functional language do, mix also accepts the switch as an optional fourth argument, mix(a, b, t, linear), and the pseudo-code for the plate uses that form so it never has to touch the global. In a pure language the step linear blending is off means "pass false", and a scenario that says nothing means "pass true". The light's way never clamps, and there's a scenario for that too: a color of 1.5 stays 1.5 until the file writer sees it.

§ 1.8 The edges of the range

Two small pictures before the big one. Both take five minutes, both are pinned by tests, and both show you something about your numbers that you'll want to have seen before chapter 10.

The ramp

Render a black-to-white ramp 256 pixels wide and 32 tall, where column x holds the light x / 255. Call it ramp. Two hundred and fifty-six evenly spaced light levels, going into a file format with 256 evenly spaced slots. You'd expect them to line up one to one. They don't even come close.

ramp():
  c ← canvas(256, 32)
  for x in 0..255:
    g ← x / 255
    for y in 0..31:
      write_pixel(c, x, y, color(g, g, g))
  return c

Look at the first line of pixel data in the file. It begins 0 0 0 13 13 13 22 22 22. The dark end of your ramp jumps by thirteen file values in its first step, then nine, then six, and the steps keep shrinking until, at column 76, two neighboring columns land on the same file value for the first time. From there to white, the file has fewer distinct values than you have columns. Count them with distinct_values: 183 of them, out of 256 slots.

This is the encoding doing exactly what it was designed to do, spending file values where your eye can use them, and it's also the reason smooth gradients band. Chapter 10 has a section on dithering, which is the fix, and it's a lot more satisfying once you've counted the problem.

The clamp

Render two patches side by side, 200 by 100 with the seam at x = 100: color(2, 0.5, 0.5) on the left and color(1, 0.25, 0.25) on the right. Call it clamp_pair. The second color is exactly half the first, so you might expect the same color at different brightnesses. Look at the file. The left patch is 255 188 188, a pale pink. The right is 255 137 137, a decidedly redder one. Clamping the red channel at 1 threw away half the red and none of the pink, and the hue changed.

Nothing to fix here. Your canvas keeps the 2, as the test insists, and only the file loses it. But when your highlights go pink in a later chapter, you'll remember why, and you'll know it isn't a bug.

§ 1.9 Putting it together

Two ramps, each mixed both ways, in one image. Black to white on top, then a red to a green chosen so the midpoint is as ugly as possible one way and as reasonable as possible the other. In each pair, the browser's way is above and the light's way beneath. A dozen lines, plus a helper that draws one pair:

ramp_pair(c, top, a, b):
  for x in 0..399:
    t ← x / 399
    naive ← mix(a, b, t, false)       // the browser's way
    light ← mix(a, b, t, true)        // the light's way
    for y in top .. top + 39:       write_pixel(c, x, y, naive)
    for y in top + 45 .. top + 84:  write_pixel(c, x, y, light)

plate_01():
  c ← canvas(400, 180)
  ramp_pair(c, 0,  color(0, 0, 0),   color(1, 1, 1))
  ramp_pair(c, 90, color(0.7, 0, 0), color(0, 0.3, 0.02))
  return c

Four bands of forty rows, starting at rows 0, 45, 90 and 135, each followed by five black rows: 40 to 44, 85 to 89 and 130 to 134 between the bands, and 175 to 179 as the bottom margin. One number worth knowing when you debug this: at x = 200, t is 200/399, which is 0.50125 and not 0.5, and that hair past the middle is why the gray midpoints land on 128 and 188 rather than teetering on 127 and 187.
